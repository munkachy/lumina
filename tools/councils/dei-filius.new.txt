#! Dei Filius — translated for Lumina from the Latin (Acta Sanctae Sedis 5, 1869–70, 481–493).

# Prologue
[Pr. 1] Pius, Bishop, Servant of the Servants of God, with the approval of the Sacred Council, for everlasting remembrance.
+ The Son of God and Redeemer of the human race, our Lord Jesus Christ, before he returned to his heavenly Father, promised that he would be with his Church on earth, still in its struggle, every day until the end of the age. So he has never once failed his beloved Bride: he stands by her when she teaches, blesses her when she works, and helps her when she is in danger. This saving care has shown itself in countless other gifts, but it has been seen most plainly in the rich harvest the Christian world has gathered from the ecumenical councils, and above all from the Council of Trent, though it met in evil times. From that Council the holy doctrines of religion were defined more exactly and explained more fully, and errors were condemned and held back. From it Church discipline was restored and made firmer; the clergy grew in learning and devotion; colleges were founded to train young men for the sacred service; and the lives of Christian people were renewed by better instruction of the faithful and more frequent use of the sacraments. From it, too, the members were bound more closely to the visible Head, and fresh vigour flowed into the whole mystical body of Christ. Religious families multiplied, along with other works of Christian devotion, and there grew that steady zeal, constant even to the shedding of blood, for spreading Christ's kingdom across the world.
[Pr. 2] We gladly and gratefully remember these and other great benefits that God in his mercy has given the Church, especially through that last ecumenical council. Yet we cannot hold back a bitter grief at the very serious evils that have come, chiefly because so many people have despised the authority of that holy Council or neglected its wise decrees.
[Pr. 3] Everyone knows what became of the heresies the Fathers of Trent condemned. Once the Church's God-given teaching office was thrown off, and matters of religion were left to each person's private judgment, those heresies slowly broke up into many sects. As these quarrelled and fought among themselves, many people in the end lost all faith in Christ. Even the Holy Bible itself, which they had once called the only source and judge of Christian teaching, they began to treat not as divine but as a collection of myths.
[Pr. 4] Then came the doctrine of rationalism, or naturalism, which has spread all too widely through the world. It sets itself against the Christian religion at every point, because that religion is supernatural in its origin, and it works with all its strength to shut Christ, our only Lord and Saviour, out of people's minds and out of the life and customs of nations, so as to set up what it calls the kingdom of pure reason, or of nature. Having left and thrown away the Christian religion, and having denied the true God and his Christ, many minds have at last fallen into the pit of pantheism, materialism and atheism. Now they deny rational nature itself, and every standard of what is just and right, and strive to tear down the deepest foundations of human society.
[Pr. 5] With this ungodliness spreading on every side, it has sadly happened that many children of the Catholic Church have also wandered from the path of true devotion, and as the truths they held were slowly whittled down, their Catholic sense grew weak. Led away by various strange teachings, and wrongly mixing up nature and grace, human knowledge and divine faith, they are found to be twisting the true meaning of the dogmas that holy Mother Church holds and teaches, and putting the wholeness and purity of the faith in danger.
[Pr. 6] When the Church sees all this, how can she not be moved to the depths of her heart? God wants all people to be saved and to come to know the truth; Christ came to save what was lost and to gather into one the scattered children of God. In the same way the Church, whom God has made the mother and teacher of the nations, knows that she is in debt to everyone. She is always ready and eager to lift up the fallen, steady those who are slipping, embrace those who come back, strengthen the good and lead them on to better things. So she can never stop bearing witness to the truth of God, which heals all things, and proclaiming it; for she knows that it was said to her: "My spirit which is upon you, and my words which I have put in your mouth, shall not depart out of your mouth, from now on and for ever" (Isaiah 59:21).
[Pr. 7] Following, then, in the footsteps of our predecessors, we have never ceased, as our supreme apostolic office requires, to teach and defend Catholic truth and to condemn false doctrines. But now the bishops of the whole world are sitting and judging with us, gathered by our authority in the Holy Spirit into this ecumenical council. Relying on the word of God, written and handed down, as we have received it from the Catholic Church, which has guarded it as holy and explained it faithfully, we have decided to profess and declare from this Chair of Peter, before all, the saving teaching of Christ, and by the power God has given us to forbid and condemn the errors opposed to it.

# Chapter 1 · God the Creator of All Things
[1] The holy, Catholic, apostolic and Roman Church believes and confesses that there is one true and living God, Creator and Lord of heaven and earth. He is almighty, eternal, beyond all measure, beyond all understanding, infinite in intellect and will and in every perfection. Since he is one, unique, completely simple and unchangeable spiritual substance, he must be proclaimed really and essentially distinct from the world, perfectly happy in himself and from himself, and raised beyond all words above everything else that exists or can be thought of.
+ This one true God, out of his goodness and by his almighty power, not to increase his happiness or to gain it, but to show his perfection through the good things he gives to creatures, by a completely free decision, at the very beginning of time made both kinds of creature out of nothing, the spiritual and the bodily, that is, the angels and the material world; and then the human creature, who shares in both, being made of spirit and body (Fourth Lateran Council).
+ And God by his providence keeps and governs everything he has made, "reaching mightily from one end of the earth to the other, and ordering all things well" (Wisdom 8:1). "All things are open and laid bare to his eyes" (see Hebrews 4:13), even those that will come about through the free action of creatures.

# Chapter 2 · Revelation
[2] The same holy Mother Church holds and teaches that God, the beginning and end of all things, can be known with certainty from created things by the natural light of human reason; for "ever since the creation of the world his invisible nature has been clearly perceived in the things that have been made" (Romans 1:20). But it pleased his wisdom and goodness to reveal himself, and the eternal decrees of his will, to the human race in another way, a supernatural way; as the Apostle says: "In many and various ways God spoke of old to our fathers by the prophets; but in these last days he has spoken to us by a Son" (Hebrews 1:1–2).
+ It is thanks to this divine revelation that those truths about God which are not in themselves beyond the reach of human reason can, even in the present condition of the human race, be known by everyone easily, with firm certainty, and with no mixture of error. Yet this is not the reason revelation must be called absolutely necessary. It is necessary because God, in his infinite goodness, has destined human beings for a supernatural end, that is, to share in the good things of God, which completely surpass what the human mind can understand; for "eye has not seen, nor ear heard, nor has it entered into the human heart, what God has prepared for those who love him" (1 Corinthians 2:9).
+ This supernatural revelation, according to the faith of the whole Church as declared by the holy Council of Trent, is contained in written books and in unwritten traditions. These traditions were received by the apostles from the lips of Christ himself, or were handed on by the apostles themselves at the prompting of the Holy Spirit, passing as it were from hand to hand until they reached us. The books of the Old and New Testaments, whole and with all their parts, as they are listed in the decree of that Council and as they are found in the old Latin Vulgate edition, are to be received as sacred and canonical. The Church holds them to be sacred and canonical not because they were put together by human effort alone and then approved by her authority; nor only because they contain revelation without error; but because, written under the inspiration of the Holy Spirit, they have God as their author, and have been handed on as such to the Church.
+ The holy Council of Trent, in order to restrain undisciplined minds, made a wise decree about the interpretation of Holy Scripture, but some people explain it wrongly. So we renew that decree and declare its meaning to be this: in matters of faith and morals that belong to the building up of Christian doctrine, the true sense of Holy Scripture is the one that holy Mother Church has held and holds, for it belongs to her to judge the true sense and interpretation of the Holy Scriptures. Therefore no one may interpret Holy Scripture against this sense, or against the unanimous agreement of the Fathers.

# Chapter 3 · Faith
[3] Human beings depend completely on God as their Creator and Lord, and created reason is wholly subject to uncreated Truth. So when God reveals, we are bound to give him, by faith, the full submission of our intellect and will. The Catholic Church professes that this faith, which is the beginning of human salvation, is a supernatural virtue. By it, with God's grace inspiring and helping us, we believe that what he has revealed is true, not because we see its inner truth by the natural light of reason, but because of the authority of God himself who reveals it, who can neither be deceived nor deceive. For faith, as the Apostle testifies, "is the assurance of things hoped for, the conviction of things not seen" (Hebrews 11:1).
+ Yet so that the submission of our faith might be in harmony with reason, God willed to join to the inner help of the Holy Spirit outward proofs of his revelation: divine deeds, above all miracles and prophecies. Since these show clearly God's almighty power and infinite knowledge, they are most certain signs of divine revelation, suited to everyone's understanding. That is why Moses and the prophets, and above all Christ the Lord himself, worked many plain miracles and made many plain prophecies. And of the apostles we read: "They went out and preached everywhere, while the Lord worked with them and confirmed the message by the signs that followed" (Mark 16:20). And again it is written: "We have the prophetic word made more sure. You will do well to pay attention to it as to a lamp shining in a dark place" (2 Peter 1:19).
+ The assent of faith is by no means a blind movement of the mind. Even so, no one can accept the preaching of the Gospel in the way needed for salvation without the light and inspiration of the Holy Spirit, who gives to all the sweetness of agreeing to the truth and believing it (Council of Orange). So faith in itself, even when it does not work through love, is a gift of God, and its act is a work that belongs to salvation. In it a person freely obeys God himself, consenting to and cooperating with his grace, which they could resist.
+ Further, everything is to be believed with divine and catholic faith that is contained in the word of God, written or handed down, and that the Church proposes for belief as divinely revealed, whether by a solemn judgment or by her ordinary and universal teaching office.
+ Since "without faith it is impossible to please God" (Hebrews 11:6) and to come into the company of his children, no one has ever been justified without it, and no one will gain eternal life unless they persevere in it to the end. And so that we might be able to fulfil our duty of embracing the true faith and of persevering in it steadily, God founded the Church through his only-begotten Son and gave her clear marks of his founding, so that she might be recognized by all as the guardian and teacher of the revealed word. For to the Catholic Church alone belong all those many and wonderful things that God has arranged to make the credibility of the Christian faith evident. Indeed the Church herself, by her marvellous spread, her outstanding holiness and her unfailing fruitfulness in every good, by her catholic unity and her unconquered stability, is a great and lasting reason for belief and an unanswerable witness to her divine mission.
+ So she is like "a sign raised up among the nations" (Isaiah 11:12). She invites to herself those who do not yet believe, and assures her children that the faith they profess rests on the firmest foundation. To this witness comes powerful help from above. For the most kind Lord stirs up and helps by his grace those who are wandering, so that they may come to know the truth; and those whom he has brought out of darkness into his wonderful light he strengthens by his grace to remain in that light, never abandoning them unless he is abandoned first. Therefore the situation of those who have held fast to Catholic truth by the heavenly gift of faith is not at all the same as that of those who, led by human opinions, follow a false religion. Those who have received the faith under the Church's teaching office can never have a just reason for changing that faith or calling it into doubt. This being so, let us give thanks to God the Father, who has made us fit to share in the inheritance of the saints in light; let us not neglect so great a salvation, but, looking to Jesus, the pioneer and perfecter of our faith, let us hold fast the confession of our hope without wavering.

# Chapter 4 · Faith and Reason
[4] The constant agreement of the Catholic Church has also held, and holds, that there are two orders of knowledge, distinct not only in their source but also in their object. In their source, because in the one we know by natural reason, in the other by divine faith. In their object, because besides the things natural reason can reach, there are proposed to us for belief mysteries hidden in God, which could not be known unless God revealed them. That is why the Apostle, who testifies that the Gentiles knew God through the things he has made, still says, when he speaks of the grace and truth that came through Jesus Christ (John 1:17): "We speak God's wisdom, hidden in a mystery, which God decreed before the ages for our glory. None of the rulers of this age understood it. But God has revealed it to us through his Spirit; for the Spirit searches everything, even the depths of God" (1 Corinthians 2:7–10). And the only-begotten Son himself praises the Father because he has hidden these things from the wise and understanding and revealed them to little ones (Matthew 11:25).
+ Reason, it is true, when it is enlightened by faith and searches carefully, devoutly and soberly, does reach, by God's gift, some understanding of the mysteries, and a most fruitful one. It does so both from the likeness they bear to the things it knows naturally, and from the way the mysteries are linked with one another and with our final end. But reason never becomes able to see through them as it sees the truths that are its own proper object. For the divine mysteries by their very nature so far exceed the created intellect that, even when they have been revealed and accepted by faith, they still remain covered by the veil of faith itself and wrapped as it were in a kind of darkness, as long as in this mortal life "we are away from the Lord; for we walk by faith, not by sight" (2 Corinthians 5:6–7).
+ But though faith is above reason, there can never be any real disagreement between faith and reason. The same God who reveals mysteries and pours in faith has put the light of reason into the human mind, and God cannot deny himself, nor can truth ever contradict truth. The empty appearance of such a contradiction comes mainly either from dogmas of faith not having been understood and explained as the Church means them, or from mere opinions being taken for the findings of reason. We therefore define that every claim contrary to the truth of enlightened faith is completely false (Fifth Lateran Council). Further, the Church, which together with the apostolic task of teaching has received the command to guard the deposit of faith, also has from God the right and the duty to condemn "what is falsely called knowledge" (1 Timothy 6:20), so that no one may be led astray "through philosophy and empty deceit" (Colossians 2:8). So all Christ's faithful are not only forbidden to defend as the lawful conclusions of science opinions which are known to be contrary to the teaching of faith, especially if the Church has condemned them; they are bound to regard them as errors wearing a false appearance of truth.
+ Not only can faith and reason never be at odds; they also help each other. Right reason shows the foundations of faith, and, lit by faith's light, develops the study of divine things. Faith frees and guards reason from errors and enriches it with knowledge of many kinds. So far, then, is the Church from opposing the cultivation of human arts and sciences that she helps and promotes it in many ways. She does not ignore or despise the benefits they bring to human life. On the contrary, she acknowledges that, as they come from God, the Lord of all knowledge, so, if they are rightly pursued, they lead to God with the help of his grace. Nor does she forbid each of these sciences to use, in its own field, its own principles and its own method. But while she recognizes this rightful freedom, she takes care that they do not take on errors by opposing divine teaching, or step beyond their proper limits and invade and unsettle what belongs to faith.
+ For the teaching of faith that God has revealed has not been offered, like some philosophical discovery, for human minds to perfect. It has been handed on as a divine deposit to the Bride of Christ, to be guarded faithfully and declared without error. So the meaning of the sacred dogmas that holy Mother Church has once declared must always be kept, and there must never be any departure from that meaning under the pretext or name of a deeper understanding. "Let understanding, knowledge and wisdom grow, then, and advance greatly and strongly, in each person and in all, in the individual and in the whole Church, through the stages of ages and centuries; but only in its own kind, that is, in the same dogma, the same meaning, the same judgment" (Vincent of Lérins).

# Canons · 1. God the Creator of All Things
[Can. 1.1] If anyone denies the one true God, Creator and Lord of things visible and invisible: let them be anathema.
[Can. 1.2] If anyone is not ashamed to claim that nothing exists except matter: let them be anathema.
[Can. 1.3] If anyone says that the substance or essence of God and of all things is one and the same: let them be anathema.
[Can. 1.4] If anyone says that finite things, whether bodily or spiritual, or at least the spiritual ones, flowed out of the divine substance; or that the divine essence becomes all things by showing or unfolding itself; or, finally, that God is universal or indefinite being, which by defining itself makes up the whole of things, divided into genera, species and individuals: let them be anathema.
[Can. 1.5] If anyone does not confess that the world and all things in it, both spiritual and material, were produced by God out of nothing in their whole substance; or says that God created not by a will free from all necessity, but as necessarily as he necessarily loves himself; or denies that the world was made for the glory of God: let them be anathema.

# Canons · 2. Revelation
[Can. 2.1] If anyone says that the one true God, our Creator and Lord, cannot be known with certainty from the things that have been made, by the natural light of human reason: let them be anathema.
[Can. 2.2] If anyone says that it is impossible, or not fitting, for human beings to be taught by divine revelation about God and the worship to be given him: let them be anathema.
[Can. 2.3] If anyone says that human beings cannot be raised by God to a knowledge and perfection beyond the natural, but can and must, by unceasing progress, at last reach by themselves the possession of all that is true and good: let them be anathema.
[Can. 2.4] If anyone does not receive as sacred and canonical the books of Holy Scripture, whole and with all their parts, as the holy Council of Trent listed them, or denies that they are divinely inspired: let them be anathema.

# Canons · 3. Faith
[Can. 3.1] If anyone says that human reason is so independent that God cannot command it to believe: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3.2] If anyone says that divine faith is not distinct from natural knowledge about God and moral matters, and so that divine faith does not require revealed truth to be believed because of the authority of God who reveals it: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3.3] If anyone says that divine revelation cannot be made credible by outward signs, and so that people must be moved to faith only by each one's inner experience or by private inspiration: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3.4] If anyone says that no miracles can happen, and so that all accounts of them, even those in Holy Scripture, must be put aside among fables and myths; or that miracles can never be known with certainty, and that the divine origin of the Christian religion is not rightly proved by them: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3.5] If anyone says that the assent of Christian faith is not free, but is necessarily produced by the arguments of human reason; or that God's grace is needed only for living faith, which works through love: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3.6] If anyone says that the situation of the faithful is the same as that of those who have not yet come to the one true faith, so that Catholics could have a just reason to suspend their assent and call into doubt the faith they have already received under the Church's teaching office, until they have completed a scientific proof of the credibility and truth of their faith: let them be anathema.

# Canons · 4. Faith and Reason
[Can. 4.1] If anyone says that divine revelation contains no true mysteries, properly so called, but that all the dogmas of faith can be understood and proved from natural principles by properly trained reason: let them be anathema.
[Can. 4.2] If anyone says that the human sciences are to be pursued with such freedom that their claims may be held as true even when they oppose revealed doctrine, and cannot be condemned by the Church: let them be anathema.
[Can. 4.3] If anyone says that it is possible that at some time, as science progresses, a sense must be given to dogmas proposed by the Church different from the sense the Church has understood and understands: let them be anathema.

# Conclusion
[End] Therefore, carrying out the duty of our supreme pastoral office, we beg all Christ's faithful, and above all those who are in charge of others or who have the task of teaching, by the tender mercy of Jesus Christ, and we command them by the authority of the same God and Saviour of ours, to give their effort and care to keeping these errors away from holy Church and driving them out, and to spreading the light of the purest faith.
+ But since it is not enough to avoid the evil of heresy unless those errors which come more or less near to it are also carefully avoided, we remind all of their duty to keep also the constitutions and decrees by which the Holy See has condemned and forbidden wrong opinions of this kind which are not listed here by name.
