#! Council of Trent, Session 6 (13 January 1547), Decree on Justification — translated for Lumina from the Latin.
#! "Righteousness" renders iustitia throughout: the Latin word behind both "justice" and "justification".

# Proem
[Proem] At this time a mistaken doctrine about justification is being spread, to the ruin of many souls and to the serious harm of the Church's unity. So the holy, ecumenical and general Council of Trent, lawfully assembled in the Holy Spirit, with the most reverend lords Giovanni Maria del Monte, Bishop of Palestrina, and Marcello, Cardinal Priest of the Holy Cross in Jerusalem, cardinals of the holy Roman Church and legates of the apostolic see, presiding in the name of our most holy father and lord in Christ, Paul III, Pope by the providence of God, intends to set out for all Christ's faithful, for the praise and glory of almighty God, the peace of the Church and the salvation of souls, the true and sound doctrine of justification. It is the doctrine that Christ Jesus, the sun of righteousness, the pioneer and perfecter of our faith, taught; that the apostles handed on; and that the Catholic Church, reminded by the Holy Spirit, has always kept. The Council strictly forbids anyone from now on to believe, preach or teach otherwise than is defined and declared in this decree.

# Chapter 1 · Nature and the Law cannot justify anyone
[1] First, the holy Council declares that, if the doctrine of justification is to be understood rightly and honestly, everyone must recognize and confess this: when all human beings lost their innocence in the sin of Adam, they became unclean and, as the Apostle says, "by nature children of wrath" (Ephesians 2:3), as the Council explained in its decree on original sin. They had become so completely slaves of sin and subject to the power of the devil and of death that neither could the Gentiles free themselves or rise up from it by the power of nature, nor could even the Jews do so by the very letter of the Law of Moses. Yet free will was by no means destroyed in them, though it was weakened in its strength and bent down.

# Chapter 2 · God's plan, and the mystery of Christ's coming
[2] So it came about that the heavenly Father, "the Father of mercies and the God of all comfort" (2 Corinthians 1:3), when the blessed fullness of time had come, sent to humanity Christ Jesus his Son, who had been made known and promised to many holy ancestors both before the Law and in the time of the Law. He sent him to redeem the Jews who were under the Law, so that the Gentiles, who were not pursuing righteousness, might lay hold of righteousness, and that all might receive adoption as children. "God put him forward as a sacrifice of atonement by his blood, effective through faith" (Romans 3:25), for our sins, and not only for ours but for the sins of the whole world (1 John 2:2).

# Chapter 3 · Who are justified through Christ
[3] But though he died for all (2 Corinthians 5:15), not all receive the benefit of his death; only those do to whom the merit of his passion is shared. People would not be born unrighteous if they were not born as descendants of Adam, since it is by that descent that they take on their own unrighteousness when they are conceived. In the same way, if they were not born again in Christ, they would never be justified, since it is by that new birth, through the merit of his passion, that they are given the grace by which they become righteous. For this blessing the Apostle urges us always to give thanks to the Father, "who has made us fit to share in the inheritance of the saints in light. He has rescued us from the power of darkness and brought us into the kingdom of his beloved Son, in whom we have redemption, the forgiveness of sins" (Colossians 1:12–14).

# Chapter 4 · What the justification of a sinner is, in outline
[4] These words give an outline of what the justification of a sinner is: a passing from the state in which a person is born a child of the first Adam into the state of grace and of adoption as a child of God through the second Adam, Jesus Christ our Saviour. Since the Gospel was proclaimed, this passing cannot happen without the washing of new birth, or the desire for it; as it is written: "Unless one is born of water and the Holy Spirit, one cannot enter the kingdom of God" (John 3:5).

# Chapter 5 · Adults must be prepared for justification, and where the preparation comes from
[5] The Council also declares that in adults the beginning of justification must come from God's prevenient grace through Christ Jesus: that is, from his call, by which they are called with no merit of their own. So those who had turned away from God by their sins are disposed, by his grace stirring them up and helping them, to turn towards their own justification by freely agreeing and cooperating with that same grace. Thus, when God touches the human heart by the light of the Holy Spirit, the person who receives that inspiration is not doing nothing at all, since they could also reject it; and yet without God's grace they cannot by their own free will move themselves towards righteousness in his sight. That is why, when Scripture says, "Turn to me, and I will turn to you" (Zechariah 1:3), we are reminded of our freedom; and when we answer, "Turn us back to you, O Lord, and we shall be turned" (Lamentations 5:21), we confess that God's grace goes before us.

# Chapter 6 · How the preparation happens
[6] People are disposed for righteousness in this way. Stirred up and helped by God's grace, receiving faith by hearing, they move freely towards God, believing that what God has revealed and promised is true, and above all that the sinner is justified by God through his grace, "through the redemption that is in Christ Jesus" (Romans 3:24). Recognizing that they are sinners, they turn from the fear of God's justice, which shakes them for their good, to think about God's mercy; and so they are lifted up to hope, trusting that God will be kind to them for Christ's sake. They begin to love him as the source of all righteousness, and so they are moved against their sins by a kind of hatred and loathing of them, that is, by the repentance that must come before baptism. Finally, they resolve to receive baptism, to begin a new life, and to keep God's commandments.
+ Of this preparation it is written: "Whoever would draw near to God must believe that he exists and that he rewards those who seek him" (Hebrews 11:6); and, "Take heart, my son; your sins are forgiven" (Matthew 9:2); and, "The fear of the Lord drives out sin" (Sirach 1:21); and, "Repent, and be baptized every one of you in the name of Jesus Christ for the forgiveness of your sins; and you will receive the gift of the Holy Spirit" (Acts 2:38); and, "Go therefore and make disciples of all nations, baptizing them in the name of the Father and of the Son and of the Holy Spirit, teaching them to observe all that I have commanded you" (Matthew 28:19–20); and finally, "Prepare your hearts for the Lord" (1 Samuel 7:3).

# Chapter 7 · What the justification of a sinner is, and its causes
[7] This disposition or preparation is followed by justification itself. Justification is not only the forgiveness of sins. It is also the making holy and the renewal of the inner person through the willing reception of grace and of the gifts. By it a person who was unrighteous becomes righteous, an enemy becomes a friend, and so becomes an heir in hope of eternal life (Titus 3:7).
+ The causes of this justification are these. The final cause, its purpose, is the glory of God and of Christ, and eternal life. The efficient cause, the one who brings it about, is the merciful God, who washes and makes holy freely (1 Corinthians 6:11), sealing and anointing "with the promised Holy Spirit, who is the pledge of our inheritance" (Ephesians 1:13–14). The meritorious cause is his most beloved only-begotten Son, our Lord Jesus Christ, who "while we were enemies" (Romans 5:10), "because of the great love with which he loved us" (Ephesians 2:4), earned justification for us by his most holy passion on the wood of the cross, and made satisfaction for us to God the Father. The instrumental cause is the sacrament of baptism, which is "the sacrament of faith", without which no one has ever been justified. Finally, the one formal cause is the righteousness of God: not the righteousness by which he himself is righteous, but the righteousness by which he makes us righteous. Given this by him, we are "renewed in the spirit of our minds" (Ephesians 4:23), and we are not only counted righteous but are truly called righteous and are righteous, receiving righteousness within us, each according to their own measure, which "the Holy Spirit gives to each one as he wills" (1 Corinthians 12:11), and according to each one's own disposition and cooperation.
+ For though no one can be righteous unless the merits of the passion of our Lord Jesus Christ are shared with them, in the justification of a sinner this happens when, by the merit of that same most holy passion, "God's love is poured into the hearts" (Romans 5:5) of those who are justified, by the Holy Spirit, and remains in them. So in justification itself, together with the forgiveness of sins, a person receives all these gifts poured in at once, through Jesus Christ into whom they are grafted: faith, hope and love. For faith, unless hope and love are added to it, neither unites a person perfectly with Christ nor makes them a living member of his body. That is why it is said most truly that "faith without works is dead" (James 2:17, 20) and idle, and that "in Christ Jesus neither circumcision nor uncircumcision is of any avail, but faith working through love" (Galatians 5:6; 6:15).
+ This faith is what catechumens ask the Church for before the sacrament of baptism, following the tradition of the apostles, when they ask for "the faith that gives eternal life". Faith cannot give that without hope and love. So they immediately hear Christ's word: "If you would enter life, keep the commandments" (Matthew 19:17). And so, as they receive true and Christian righteousness, they are told, as soon as they are born again, to keep it white and spotless, like a first robe given them through Christ Jesus in place of the one that Adam lost for himself and for us by his disobedience, so that they may carry it before the judgment seat of our Lord Jesus Christ and have eternal life.

# Chapter 8 · How "justified by faith" and "justified freely" are to be understood
[8] When the Apostle says that a person is justified "by faith" and "freely" (Romans 3:22–24), his words must be understood in the sense the constant agreement of the Catholic Church has held and expressed. We are said to be justified by faith because faith is "the beginning of human salvation", the foundation and root of all justification, "without which it is impossible to please God" (Hebrews 11:6) and to come into the company of his children. We are said to be justified freely because nothing that comes before justification, whether faith or works, earns the grace of justification itself. For "if it is by grace, it is no longer on the basis of works; otherwise", as the same Apostle says, "grace would no longer be grace" (Romans 11:6).

# Chapter 9 · Against the empty confidence of heretics
[9] It is necessary to believe that sins are not forgiven, and never have been forgiven, except freely, by God's mercy, for Christ's sake. Yet it must not be said that anyone's sins are forgiven, or have been forgiven, simply because they boast of being confident and certain that their sins are forgiven, and rely on that alone. For this empty confidence, far from all true devotion, can exist among heretics and schismatics; indeed in our time it does exist, and is preached with great hostility against the Catholic Church.
+ Nor should it be claimed that those who are truly justified must be utterly convinced in their own minds, without any doubt at all, that they are justified; or that no one is absolved from sins and justified except the one who firmly believes that they are absolved and justified; or that absolution and justification are brought about by this belief alone, as though anyone who does not believe it were doubting God's promises and the power of Christ's death and resurrection. No devout person should doubt God's mercy, the merit of Christ, or the power and effect of the sacraments. But in the same way, anyone may be afraid and uneasy about their own state of grace when they look at themselves and their own weakness and unreadiness. For no one can know with the certainty of faith, a certainty that cannot be mistaken, that they have received God's grace.

# Chapter 10 · The growth of justification once received
[10] So those who have been justified in this way, and made friends of God and members of his household (John 15:15; Ephesians 2:19), go "from strength to strength" (Psalm 84:7) and are renewed, as the Apostle says, "day by day" (2 Corinthians 4:16): that is, by putting to death what is earthly in them (Colossians 3:5) and offering their members as weapons of righteousness for holiness (Romans 6:13, 19). Through keeping the commandments of God and of the Church, with faith working together with good works, they grow in the very righteousness they received through Christ's grace, and are justified still more. As it is written: "Let the righteous still do right" (Revelation 22:11); and, "Do not wait until death to be justified" (Sirach 18:22); and again, "You see that a person is justified by works and not by faith alone" (James 2:24). Holy Church asks for this growth in righteousness when she prays: "Give us, Lord, an increase of faith, hope and love."

# Chapter 11 · Keeping the commandments: that it is necessary, and that it is possible
[11] No one, however justified, should think that they are free from keeping the commandments. No one should use that rash saying, which the Fathers condemned under anathema, that God's commandments are impossible for a justified person to keep. "For God does not command the impossible; but when he commands, he tells you to do what you can, and to ask for what you cannot, and he helps you so that you can" (Augustine). "His commandments are not burdensome" (1 John 5:3); "his yoke is easy and his burden light" (Matthew 11:30). Those who are children of God love Christ; and those who love him keep his words, as he himself testifies (John 14:23), and with God's help they certainly can.
+ For in this mortal life even the holiest and most righteous sometimes fall into at least light and everyday sins, which are also called venial, but they do not stop being righteous because of this. "Forgive us our debts" (Matthew 6:12) is the humble and honest prayer of the righteous. So the righteous should feel all the more bound to walk in the way of righteousness, because, set free from sin and made servants of God (Romans 6:22), living "soberly, righteously and devoutly" (Titus 2:12), they can make progress through Christ Jesus, through whom they have gained access to this grace (Romans 5:2). For God does not abandon by his grace those he has once justified, unless he is abandoned by them first.
+ So no one should flatter themselves with faith alone, thinking that by faith alone they have been made an heir and will receive the inheritance, even if they do not suffer with Christ in order to be glorified with him (Romans 8:17). For even Christ himself, as the Apostle says, "although he was a Son, learned obedience through what he suffered; and being made perfect he became the source of eternal salvation for all who obey him" (Hebrews 5:8–9). That is why the Apostle himself warns those who are justified: "Do you not know that in a race all the runners compete, but only one receives the prize? So run that you may obtain it. So I do not run aimlessly, I do not box as one beating the air; but I discipline my body and keep it under control, lest after preaching to others I myself should be disqualified" (1 Corinthians 9:24–27). And Peter, the chief of the apostles, says: "Be all the more eager to confirm your call and election by good works; for if you do this you will never fall" (2 Peter 1:10).
+ It is clear, then, that those people oppose the teaching of the true faith who say that the righteous sin at least venially in every good work, or, what is even harder to bear, that they deserve eternal punishment. So also do those who hold that the righteous sin in all their works if, in doing them, they look also to the eternal reward as well as, first of all, to God's glory, as a way of rousing themselves from sloth and urging themselves to run the race. For it is written: "I have inclined my heart to do your statutes because of the reward" (Psalm 119:112, Vulgate); and the Apostle says of Moses that "he looked to the reward" (Hebrews 11:26).

# Chapter 12 · Rash presumption about predestination must be avoided
[12] As long as we live this mortal life, no one should presume so far upon the hidden mystery of God's predestination as to decide for certain that they are definitely among the predestined; as if it were true that a justified person either cannot sin any more, or, if they do sin, can promise themselves sure repentance. For, except by a special revelation, it cannot be known whom God has chosen for himself.

# Chapter 13 · The gift of perseverance
[13] The same holds for the gift of perseverance, of which it is written: "The one who endures to the end will be saved" (Matthew 10:22; 24:13). This gift can come from no one except him "who is able to make stand" the one who stands (Romans 14:4), so that they stand to the end, and to raise up again the one who falls. Let no one promise themselves anything certain about it with absolute certainty, though all ought to place and rest the firmest hope in God's help. For God, unless they themselves fail his grace, will finish the good work as he began it, working in them both to will and to accomplish (Philippians 1:6; 2:13).
+ Yet those who think they stand should take care that they do not fall (1 Corinthians 10:12), and should work out their salvation "with fear and trembling" (Philippians 2:12): in labours, in vigils, in almsgiving, in prayers and offerings, in fasting and chastity (2 Corinthians 6:5–6). They ought to be afraid, knowing that they have been born again to the hope of glory (1 Peter 1:3) and not yet to glory, because of the fight that still lies ahead with the flesh, the world and the devil. In that fight they cannot win unless with God's grace they obey the Apostle, who says: "We are debtors, not to the flesh, to live according to the flesh; for if you live according to the flesh you will die, but if by the Spirit you put to death the deeds of the body you will live" (Romans 8:12–13).

# Chapter 14 · Those who have fallen, and their restoration
[14] Those who have fallen by sin from the grace of justification they received can be justified again if, with God stirring them up, they take care to recover the grace they lost, through the sacrament of penance and by the merit of Christ. This way of justification is the restoration of one who has fallen, which the holy Fathers have fittingly called "a second plank after the shipwreck of grace lost". For Christ Jesus instituted the sacrament of penance for those who fall into sin after baptism, when he said: "Receive the Holy Spirit. If you forgive the sins of any, they are forgiven; if you retain the sins of any, they are retained" (John 20:22–23).
+ So it must be taught that the repentance of a Christian after a fall is very different from repentance at baptism. It includes not only stopping sinning and hating sin, "a broken and contrite heart" (Psalm 51:17), but also the sacramental confession of those sins, at least in desire, to be made at the proper time, and priestly absolution; and also satisfaction, through fasting, almsgiving, prayer and the other devout practices of the spiritual life. This satisfaction is not for the eternal punishment, which is forgiven together with the guilt, either by the sacrament or by the desire for the sacrament. It is for the temporal punishment which, as Holy Scripture teaches, is not always forgiven completely, as it is in baptism, to those who have been ungrateful for the grace of God they received, "have grieved the Holy Spirit" (Ephesians 4:30), and have not been afraid to violate God's temple (1 Corinthians 3:17). Of this repentance it is written: "Remember from where you have fallen; repent, and do the works you did at first" (Revelation 2:5); and again, "Godly grief produces a repentance that leads to salvation and brings no regret" (2 Corinthians 7:10); and again, "Repent, and bear fruit worthy of repentance" (Matthew 3:2, 8; Luke 3:8).

# Chapter 15 · Every mortal sin loses grace, but not faith
[15] Against the cunning minds of certain people who "by smooth talk and flattery deceive the hearts of the simple" (Romans 16:18), it must also be stated that the grace of justification once received is lost not only by unbelief, by which faith itself is lost, but also by any other mortal sin, even though faith is not lost. This defends the teaching of God's law, which shuts out of the kingdom of God not only unbelievers but also believers who are "fornicators, adulterers, the effeminate, men who lie with men, thieves, the greedy, drunkards, slanderers, robbers" (1 Corinthians 6:9–10), and all others who commit deadly sins. With the help of God's grace they can keep from these sins, and by them they are cut off from the grace of Christ.

# Chapter 16 · The fruit of justification: the merit of good works, and what that merit is
[16] So to those who have been justified in this way, whether they have kept without a break the grace they received or have recovered it after losing it, the words of the Apostle should be set before them: "Abound in every good work, knowing that in the Lord your labour is not in vain" (1 Corinthians 15:58); "for God is not so unjust as to forget your work and the love you have shown for his name" (Hebrews 6:10); and, "Do not throw away your confidence, which has a great reward" (Hebrews 10:35).
+ And so to those who do good to the end (Matthew 10:22) and hope in God, eternal life is to be held out both as a grace mercifully promised to the children of God through Christ Jesus, and as a reward which, by God's own promise, will be faithfully given for their good works and merits (Augustine). This is that "crown of righteousness" which the Apostle said was laid up for him after his fight and his race, to be given him by the righteous Judge; and not only to him, but to all who have loved his appearing (2 Timothy 4:7–8).
+ For Christ Jesus himself constantly pours his strength into those who are justified, as the head into the members (Ephesians 4:15) and the vine into the branches (John 15:5). This strength always goes before their good works, goes with them, and follows them; without it those works could in no way be pleasing to God or meritorious. So we must believe that nothing further is lacking to those who are justified. They are to be considered to have fully satisfied God's law, as far as the state of this life allows, by those works that have been done in God; and to have truly earned eternal life, to be received in its due time, provided they die in grace (Revelation 14:13). For Christ our Saviour says: "Whoever drinks of the water that I shall give will never thirst; the water that I shall give will become in them a spring of water welling up to eternal life" (John 4:14).
+ So our own righteousness is not set up as coming from ourselves (Romans 10:3; 2 Corinthians 3:5), nor is the righteousness of God ignored or rejected. For the righteousness that is called ours, because we are justified by it as it remains in us, is that very same righteousness of God, because God pours it into us through the merit of Christ.
+ Nor must this be left out. Holy Scripture values good works so highly that Christ promises that whoever gives even a cup of cold water to one of his least ones will not lose their reward (Matthew 10:42), and the Apostle testifies that "this slight momentary affliction is preparing for us an eternal weight of glory beyond all comparison" (2 Corinthians 4:17). Even so, a Christian must never trust in or boast of themselves instead of in the Lord (1 Corinthians 1:31; 2 Corinthians 10:17), whose goodness towards all people is so great that he wills his own gifts to be their merits.
+ And since "we all make many mistakes" (James 3:2), each of us must keep before our eyes God's severity and judgment as well as his mercy and goodness. No one should judge themselves, even if they are aware of nothing against themselves; for the whole of human life is to be examined and judged not by human judgment but by God's, "who will bring to light the things now hidden in darkness and disclose the purposes of the heart; then each one will receive their praise from God" (1 Corinthians 4:4–5), who, as it is written, "will repay each one according to their works" (Romans 2:6).

# Canons on Justification
[Canons] After this Catholic doctrine of justification, which no one can be justified without faithfully and firmly accepting, it has pleased the holy Council to add these canons, so that all may know not only what they must hold and follow, but also what they must avoid and shun.
[Can. 1] If anyone says that a person can be justified before God by their own works, done either by the powers of human nature or through the teaching of the Law, without God's grace through Jesus Christ: let them be anathema.
[Can. 2] If anyone says that God's grace through Christ Jesus is given only so that a person may live righteously and earn eternal life more easily, as though by free will without grace they could do both, though with hardship and difficulty: let them be anathema.
[Can. 3] If anyone says that without the prevenient inspiration and help of the Holy Spirit a person can believe, hope, love or repent as they ought, so that the grace of justification may be given them: let them be anathema.
[Can. 4] If anyone says that a person's free will, moved and stirred up by God, does nothing to cooperate by consenting to God, who stirs it up and calls it, in disposing and preparing itself to obtain the grace of justification; and that it cannot refuse if it wishes, but, like something lifeless, does nothing at all and is merely passive: let them be anathema.
[Can. 5] If anyone says that after Adam's sin human free will was lost and destroyed; or that it is a thing in name only, or rather a name with no thing behind it; or, finally, a fiction brought into the Church by Satan: let them be anathema.
[Can. 6] If anyone says that it is not in a person's power to make their ways evil, but that God does evil works just as he does good ones, not only by permitting them but properly and in himself, so that the betrayal of Judas is no less his own work than the calling of Paul: let them be anathema.
[Can. 7] If anyone says that all works done before justification, for whatever reason they are done, are truly sins or deserve God's hatred; or that the harder a person tries to dispose themselves for grace, the more seriously they sin: let them be anathema.
[Can. 8] If anyone says that the fear of hell, by which we flee to God's mercy in sorrow for our sins or keep from sinning, is a sin or makes sinners worse: let them be anathema.
[Can. 9] If anyone says that the sinner is justified by faith alone, meaning that nothing else is required to cooperate in obtaining the grace of justification, and that it is in no way necessary for them to be prepared and disposed by a movement of their own will: let them be anathema.
[Can. 10] If anyone says that people are justified without the righteousness of Christ, by which he earned merit for us; or that they are formally righteous by that righteousness itself: let them be anathema.
[Can. 11] If anyone says that people are justified either by the imputation of Christ's righteousness alone, or by the forgiveness of sins alone, to the exclusion of the grace and love that is poured into their hearts by the Holy Spirit and remains in them; or that the grace by which we are justified is only God's favour: let them be anathema.
[Can. 12] If anyone says that justifying faith is nothing other than confidence in God's mercy, which forgives sins for Christ's sake; or that it is this confidence alone by which we are justified: let them be anathema.
[Can. 13] If anyone says that to obtain the forgiveness of sins everyone must believe with certainty, and without any hesitation because of their own weakness and unreadiness, that their sins have been forgiven: let them be anathema.
[Can. 14] If anyone says that a person is absolved from their sins and justified because they firmly believe that they are absolved and justified; or that no one is truly justified except the one who believes they are justified, and that absolution and justification are brought about by this belief alone: let them be anathema.
[Can. 15] If anyone says that a person who is born again and justified is bound by faith to believe that they are certainly among the predestined: let them be anathema.
[Can. 16] If anyone says, with absolute and infallible certainty, that they will certainly have that great gift of perseverance to the end, unless they have learned this by a special revelation: let them be anathema.
[Can. 17] If anyone says that the grace of justification comes only to those predestined to life, and that all the others who are called are called indeed but do not receive grace, since they are predestined to evil by God's power: let them be anathema.
[Can. 18] If anyone says that God's commandments are impossible to keep, even for one who is justified and living under grace: let them be anathema.
[Can. 19] If anyone says that nothing is commanded in the Gospel except faith, and that everything else is indifferent, neither commanded nor forbidden but left free; or that the Ten Commandments have nothing to do with Christians: let them be anathema.
[Can. 20] If anyone says that a justified person, however perfect, is not bound to keep the commandments of God and of the Church, but only to believe, as though the Gospel were a bare and unconditional promise of eternal life without the condition of keeping the commandments: let them be anathema.
[Can. 21] If anyone says that Christ Jesus was given by God to humanity as a redeemer to trust in, and not also as a lawgiver to obey: let them be anathema.
[Can. 22] If anyone says that a justified person can persevere in the righteousness they have received without God's special help, or that with it they cannot: let them be anathema.
[Can. 23] If anyone says that a person once justified cannot sin any more or lose grace, and so that one who falls and sins was never truly justified; or, on the other hand, that a person can avoid all sins, even venial ones, through their whole life, except by a special privilege from God, as the Church holds of the Blessed Virgin: let them be anathema.
[Can. 24] If anyone says that the righteousness received is not kept, and also not increased, before God through good works, but that the works themselves are only the fruits and signs of justification already obtained, and not also a cause of its increase: let them be anathema.
[Can. 25] If anyone says that the righteous sin in every good work, at least venially, or, what is harder to bear, mortally, and so deserve eternal punishment, and that they are not condemned only because God does not count those works towards condemnation: let them be anathema.
[Can. 26] If anyone says that the righteous should not expect and hope for an eternal reward from God, through his mercy and the merit of Jesus Christ, for the good works done in God, if they persevere to the end in doing good and keeping the divine commandments: let them be anathema.
[Can. 27] If anyone says that there is no mortal sin except unbelief, or that grace once received is lost by no other sin, however grave and enormous, except unbelief: let them be anathema.
[Can. 28] If anyone says that when grace is lost through sin, faith is always lost with it; or that the faith that remains is not true faith, even though it is not living faith; or that one who has faith without love is not a Christian: let them be anathema.
[Can. 29] If anyone says that one who has fallen after baptism cannot rise again by God's grace; or that they can indeed recover the righteousness they lost, but by faith alone, without the sacrament of penance, as the holy Roman and universal Church, taught by Christ the Lord and his apostles, has until now professed, kept and taught: let them be anathema.
[Can. 30] If anyone says that after the grace of justification has been received, the guilt of every repentant sinner is forgiven and the debt of eternal punishment wiped out in such a way that no debt of temporal punishment remains to be paid, either in this world or in the next in purgatory, before entrance into the kingdom of heaven can be opened: let them be anathema.
[Can. 31] If anyone says that a justified person sins when they do good with an eye to the eternal reward: let them be anathema.
[Can. 32] If anyone says that the good works of a justified person are gifts of God in such a way that they are not also the good merits of the justified person; or that the justified person, by the good works they do through God's grace and the merit of Jesus Christ, of whom they are a living member, does not truly merit an increase of grace, eternal life, and, provided they die in grace, the attainment of that eternal life, and also an increase of glory: let them be anathema.
[Can. 33] If anyone says that by this Catholic doctrine of justification, set out by the holy Council in this present decree, the glory of God or the merits of Jesus Christ our Lord are in any way lessened, and not rather that the truth of our faith, and indeed the glory of God and of Christ Jesus, are made to shine more brightly: let them be anathema.
