Nothing is written yet. This is the document to argue with. Every want below is a claim I am making about why somebody does something, and any one of them you reject is cheap to change now and expensive to change in eleven thousand words of prose.

The one-sentence premise

Forty-one people are living in a closed parish school with nine days of quiet left, and you are the one deciding what gets built with the time.

Super-objective (yours, all game): I want these people to still be here at the end of the winter. Every scene want below is a local version of that.

What makes it hard: you cannot build everything, you do not know what is coming, and the work you spend finding out what is coming is work you did not spend on the wall.

The place

St. Basil's, a Catholic grade school closed in 2009, and the church attached to it by a covered walk. Brick. Chain-link on three sides and a loading dock on the fourth. A gym, a kitchen with two gas ranges and no gas, a boiler room, a nurse's office, a playground with four inches of topsoil under the asphalt, a dry well the parish capped in 1988, and a bell tower with a view down the highway to the county line.

Forty-one people. Nine days before the weather turns and the roads move.

The ten projects — the only state this game keeps

Scouting costs work too. Each of the four scouting trips costs 1 day and tells you which crisis is likeliest. That is the central decision of the game: eleven days of building and no idea, or eight days of building and a good guess.

The four choices that no wall can answer

These sit outside the build and cannot be solved by it. This is where the story is.

  1. Day two — nine people at the gate. Nine more pairs of hands, nine more mouths, and a winter that was already tight. Fr. Nowak wants them in. Ruthie wants a number.
  2. Day six — Dell's hands. He is failing and hiding it and the wall crew is his. He wants to finish the job. You want the footings to hold.
  3. The crisis — somebody is outside when the gate has to shut. Nine seconds is the design spec you either built or did not.
  4. After — one person is sick and you have four doses. Ruthie will do it if you tell her to, and she is asking you not to make her decide.

The build menu — how spending works

At scenes 3 and 7 you get the same list with the costs and the days remaining, and you take projects until the days run out. That is one node with a conditional list, not thirty branches. Each purchase sets a flag and subtracts. Scenes 5 and any other scouting trip take from the same pot.

Nothing is hidden. The costs, the days left, and what each project is believed to be good for are all on the page. The unknown is which crisis comes, and that is what scouting is for.

Open questions

What I would like you to reject

Places where I have guessed and would rather be told:

  1. Twelve days against thirty-one days of work. That is a 39% budget. It might want to be 45% — tight enough to hurt, loose enough that two good guesses feel like a plan.
  2. Five crises may be one too many for 11,000 words. Four gives every crisis more room.
  3. Fr. Nowak's want. To keep them a parish and not a garrison is the most interesting thing about him and also the thing most likely to be wrong. He could equally want to leave, or to have died in the first week and be ashamed of not having.
  4. Whether the priest should be right. The chapel project costs four days — as much as the wall. If it never pays off, it is a tax on piety. If it always pays off, it is a sermon. My instinct is that it pays off in exactly two of the five crises and everyone can see which.
