*comment ---- 3.2 : HALINA. 1982, age seven ----

Two years later, and the thing you notice first about Halina Kowalczyk is that she is funny on purpose.

Not warm, exactly. Warm is not the word. She is eighty-four, she has buried a husband and a country, she has opinions about the Pope, the new priest, her daughter-in-law's soup, and a neighbour called Mrs. Duda who she says has been dying since 1961 and is very good at it.

She says all this in front of Adam, in Polish and in English, and he thinks she is the funniest person alive, which she is.

*page_break

And she is teaching him to pray.

She does it badly, in the sense that she has no idea what she is doing and no training whatsoever. She does it well, in the sense that it is working.

She kneels him down beside the bed at seven o'clock. She makes him say the words out loud because *God is not a mind reader*, which is theologically wrong and which she believes with her whole chest. She tells him that when you cannot think of anything to say you say the same thing again, and that this is not cheating.

*page_break

She also tells him two things that are not true.

The first is small and you will forget about it.

The second she tells him in October, sitting on the edge of the bed with her hand flat on the blanket, and it is this: that God is keeping a list, and that the list is very detailed, and that the whole business of confession is getting the list right.

She does not say it to frighten him. She says it the way you would explain a bus timetable. She is eighty-four years old and this is what somebody told her in 1908 in a village that does not exist any more, and it has held her up through two wars, and she is handing it on because it is the best thing she has.

*comment ---- 3.3 : THE FINGER. 1984, age nine ----

You are going to want to pay attention to this one.

It is a Thursday in February and the kitchen on Dobson Street is too warm because the radiator has one setting. Adam is nine. Then he picks the glass back up.

*page_break

"What was that?" you say.

Your angel does not answer, because your angel is watching, and you have learned by now that when it goes quiet it is because it is seeing all of a thing at once and there is nothing to add.

So you watch too.

He does it again at four o'clock, reaching for a book. He does it at twenty past five with a fork. He does it at bedtime, twice, once because of a real one and once because he is not sure whether the first one counted.

*page_break

Here is what is happening, and neither of you can see it, because it is behind the door.

He made the sign. He did not mean to. It does not matter that he did not mean to, because he is nine and nobody has explained to him that meaning to is the whole of it, and what he was told at seven was that there is a list and the business is getting the list right.

So: he made the sign. That is on the list. If he does not undo it he will have to confess it. If he forgets to confess it, then next time he goes up for communion he will be going up with it still on him.

Pointing at the floor undoes it. He worked this out himself. Nobody taught him this and nobody could talk him out of it because nobody knows.

*page_break

He does it forty times a day.

He does it for four years.

His mother never notices, because he is careful, and because she is working, and because he is a quiet boy and quiet boys are the ones you stop looking at.

*comment ---- 3.4 : THE MILL AND THE LOFT. 1985, age ten ----

In 1985 the mill on the South Side closes.

Stan Kowalczyk is forty-eight and has been there since he was nineteen and there is not going to be work like that again, not for him, not on that river, not ever. Half of Dobson Street goes over the next four years. The bakery on Liberty holds on until 1991.

He does not say anything about it.

That is the thing you have to understand about this man and you will be understanding it for another thirty-one years. He is not cold. He is not absent. He is a man who was raised by a man who did not speak, and the machinery for saying a thing out loud was never installed, and there is nothing to be done about it now.

*page_break

So instead, on the second Saturday after, he goes up onto the roof.

There is a structure up there that has been shut since 1971. It was his father's. It is a pigeon loft — a proper one, cedar, with a landing board and a trap and forty-eight nest boxes — and half of Polish Hill had one and most of them are gone now.

He cleans it out. It takes him two weekends. He does not explain what he is doing and nobody asks.

*page_break

Then he brings Adam up the ladder and stands there with his hands in his pockets and says:

"It's yours."

And that is all he says. That is the entire ceremony. A man who cannot tell his son one true thing about the world has just handed him a roof full of birds instead, and it is the largest thing he will ever say to anybody.

*page_break

*comment ---- the birds : the angel's one competence ----

Here is what you find out over the next three years, and it is the single funniest thing that happens in forty-nine years.

Not *nudge*. Not *arrange circumstances*. A pigeon has no free will — there is no door in the middle of it — and so the whole of an angelic will can be applied to a pigeon and the pigeon does what it is told, completely, at four hundred miles.
