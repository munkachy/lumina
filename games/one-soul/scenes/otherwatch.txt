Eleven days after the classroom door, Renee Sobek's guardian speaks to yours for the first time in thirteen years.

It happens in a doorway on Dobson Street at about four in the afternoon. There is nothing ceremonious about it. It simply hands something over — instantly, entire, no preamble, the way they do — and your angel receives it, and then stands there holding it.

"What," you say. "What was that."

"A fact about the girl."

"What fact?"

"Her father drinks," it says. "Not visibly. Not yet. It has been getting worse since June and there is a particular kind of evening in that house now, about twice a week, and she has begun to be able to tell in the first ten seconds which kind of evening it is going to be."

*page_break

You look at the other angel.

It is not like yours. That is the first thing. It is not *unlike* yours either — you cannot say it is bigger or smaller or older, because none of those are things it has — but it is a different kind of creature entirely, and the word that keeps coming to you is one you would be embarrassed to say out loud, and the word is *severe.*

"Ask it what it wants," you say.

*page_break

What it wants is this.

Renee should pull back from Adam. Not cruelly and not all at once. Over the next year she should find other people, and take up the things she is good at, and stop spending four afternoons a week on a roof three doors down.

Because it has been watching him too. It can see, from outside, exactly what you can see: a boy who has been closing since October. And a thirteen-year-old girl who attaches herself to a boy who is closing will spend the next ten years of her life waiting for a door to open.

Her good is that she should not spend her adolescence waiting.

*page_break

And Adam's good is that Renee Sobek is the only thing currently keeping him in the world.

Since October he has stopped talking to two other boys, stopped going to the club on Saturdays, and started spending an unusual amount of time on a roof by himself. There is exactly one person he still says whole sentences to, and she is thirteen years old, and she does not know she is doing it, and if she goes he will not follow her.

*page_break

Neither of them is wrong.

That is the thing you are going to have to sit with, and it takes you about a minute and a half, and you do not like it at the end of the minute and a half either.

There is no arbitration. Nobody is coming to decide between them. The two of them are under the same obedience and they are obeying it, and they are on opposite sides of one afternoon on Dobson Street.

"That's it? It just — knows? It doesn't have somebody in a *bed* telling it —"

"No."

"How long does it take?"

"There is no time in it," your angel says. "It is not told and then it decides. It is told, and that is the whole of it, and then it acts."

*page_break

You sit with that. Or you would sit with it, if there were anything here to sit on.

Somewhere in the last thirteen years you have started to think of this arrangement as an honour. You would not have said so out loud. But you had begun, quietly, to think that being handed an angel to direct was a thing that had been given to you because of something about you.

And here is another one, doing the same job, being told directly by God, instantly, with no woman in a bed in Pittsburgh in the way.
