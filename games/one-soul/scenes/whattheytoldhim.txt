*comment ---- 6.2 : THE SATURDAY LINE. 1989–1992 ----

He goes.

Of course he goes. He is the boy who pointed at the floor forty times a day for four years. On the second Saturday he is in the line at St. Casimir with eleven other people, mostly women over sixty, and he says it, and he cannot get the words out properly, and it takes him three tries.

And then he walks home.

And halfway home, at the corner of Brereton and Herron, he is not certain he said it *properly.*

*page_break

So he goes back the following week and says it again.

And the week after that.

*page_break

This is the machine. It is worth looking at squarely, because it runs for four years and it is the reason for everything that comes after.

The scrupulosity was already there — that is 1984, that is the finger, that is an instrument that got bent before anybody could have known. What has happened now is that the bent instrument has been handed something that genuinely *is* serious, and it is doing what a bent instrument does with a real weight: it is grinding.

He is not lax. He is the opposite of lax. He is fifteen years old and going to confession every week over one thing and never once feeling that it has taken.

*comment ---- 6.7 : THE RELIEF. 1993 ----

He stops going in the autumn.

And here is the part nobody tells you, and the part this is going to insist on: **it is wonderful.**

*page_break

No more Dennis Wisniewski by the coffee urn. No more standing in a line on a Saturday afternoon performing an accounting he has never once got right. No more lying in bed on a Friday going back over the week with a pencil.

And the nine o'clock at St. Casimir in 1993 is, objectively, bad. This is not a slander and it is not a set-up for a correction later. There is a cantor who cannot sing and knows it and is doing her best. There is a homily read off a photocopy, badly, by a man who is exhausted. There are eighty-one people in a building built for six hundred, in coats, in October, because the boiler is out again.

And there are four people in the third pew who were unkind to his mother in 1987 in a way he has not forgotten, and every single Sunday of his life he has watched them go up and come back with their hands folded.

*page_break

He does not decide anything.

That is the other part. There is no scene, no argument, no door. He does not go one Sunday because he is tired. He does not go the next because he did not go the last. By November it is simply a thing he does not do, and nobody in the house says one word about it.

*page_break

He is eighteen years old and he is free, and it feels *exactly* like being free, and some of his reasons are good reasons.
