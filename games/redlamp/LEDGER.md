# THE RED LAMP — canon ledger

Small on purpose. One building, one night, four people. Everything in this file
is something the reader can stand in, smell, or trip over. Nothing in it is
history that has to be explained.

---

## The place

**St. Wenceslaus, Braddock Avenue, Pittsburgh.** Slovak parish, brick, built
1897 by mill families who paid for it in instalments off the payroll. The mill
closed in 1982. The parish has been merged twice and is now one of four served
by one priest.

Physical facts, used consistently:

- **The doors.** Two sets. The outer doors are oak and stand open until the
  sacristan locks them at midnight. Between them and the nave is a vestibule
  with a terrazzo floor, a rack of missalettes, and **the font** — a granite
  bowl on a pillar, filled every Saturday, directly in the path.
- **The nave.** Twenty-two pews on each side, dark. One light left on over the
  choir loft stair. It smells of candle wax, radiator dust, and lemon oil.
- **The east window.** Christ knocking at a door, given in 1924 by the Hlavatý
  family. Faces east down Braddock Avenue. **Sunrise strikes it at 6:14.**
- **The sanctuary.** Three steps, an altar rail nobody removed, the tabernacle
  behind, and above it **the red lamp** — a wick in red glass, burning because
  He is there. It has not gone out since 1897 except once, in a power cut, and
  Verna remembers it.
- **The confessional.** Wooden, on the epistle side. Unused for face-to-face
  confession in fifteen years. A purple stole hangs on a nail inside the priest's
  door. It smells of old varnish.
- **The sacristy.** Behind the sanctuary on the gospel side. Vesting case,
  credence table, a sink called **the sacrarium** that drains into the ground,
  the ablution cup, an electric kettle, a wall safe, and a shelf of **parish
  registers** in green cloth boards, one volume per decade back to 1897.
- **The tower stair.** Off the vestibule. Ninety-one steps. **The bell** was
  rung by rope until 1974 and has not been rung since; the rope is still there.
- **The undercroft.** Down from the sacristy. Furnace, folding tables, a
  refrigerator that hums, and behind a plywood partition **the founding pastor's
  vault**, Fr. Jozef Kubica, d. 1931. No windows. Nothing down there sees dawn.
- **The parking lot.** Ten spaces, sodium light, a chain-link fence, and beyond
  it Braddock Avenue and the river.

**Time.** Thursday, 11:04 p.m. to 6:14 a.m. Late October, wet.

## You

**He has not forgotten anything.** That is the point and it must never read as
amnesia, which is not what happens to people and is not what happened to him.

He remembers all of it, in detail: hanging the bell in 1928, the beer Fr. Kubica
sent up at four o'clock, his mother's hands, the boarding house on Talbot
Avenue. What he lost is not the memories. It is the ability to make the word
*I* reach back to the boy in them. Ondrej Hlavatý is somebody he remembers
extremely well, the way you remember a friend who died — and the connection
between that boy and the thing sitting in the back pew came apart at some point
between 1931 and about 1954, and will not hold for more than a few seconds at a
time.

So he can tell you everything about Ondrej Hlavatý and cannot say *I am*.

The register in the sacristy is what breaks that, because a document is a
witness. Somebody with a good hand wrote both halves on one line in 1904 and it
has been sitting on a shelf ever since, and you cannot argue with a book:

> **Hlavatý, Ondrej Michal — b. 14 Aug 1904 — bapt. 21 Aug 1904 — par. Ján
> Hlavatý & Anna (Sokolová) — spon. Michal Sokol, Terézia Bártová**

That is also why an act of contrition is gated behind it. Contrition needs a
first person. He could not say *I have sinned* while *I* would not reach.

**What he wants — the super-objective.** In about 1911, in this building,
Fr. Jozef Kubica preached on the Eucharist in Slovak and used the old phrase
from Ignatius of Antioch: *liek nesmrteľnosti*, the medicine of immortality,
the antidote so that we should not die but live for ever. Ondrej was seven and
sitting on the epistle side and remembers the whole afternoon.

He has carried that sentence for ninety-four years as the one hope he has. He
has a false immortality. There is a medicine of the true one, four streets from
where he was born, and it is in a brass box at the end of this building.

**He is right about the medicine and wrong about the dose.** He cannot make
himself go up those three steps, so on any night when he is desperate enough he
reaches for something adjacent and available instead — the rinse water, the
cup, the brass door — and none of those is the thing, and every one of them is a
way of not walking up the aisle.

Marek made you in April 1931, in a boarding house on Talbot Avenue, while you
were dying of an influenza that took four other men in the same house. You were
twenty-seven. **He is not lying about that and never lies about it.**

Ninety-four years. You know what you have done and you have stopped counting.

Tonight you were driven off Braddock Avenue by two of your own kind and you went
through the first unlocked door you found.

## Verna Sowka

Seventy-nine. Thursday nights, eleven to midnight, for thirty-one years — the
parish signed up for perpetual adoration in 1994, the roster collapsed inside
two years, and Verna kept her hour.

Widow. Two sons, one in Ohio and one who does not call. Bad left hip. Comes in
a plastic rain bonnet. Sits in the fourth pew on the Mary side, not the front,
because the front is for people who want to be seen. Brings a thermos.

**She is frightened of you.** Properly, physically frightened, from the first
moment, and she stays. That is not the same as being unafraid and it is worth
more.

She has a rosary with one decade of the beads worn to the string. She will offer
it. She calls you *son* before she knows anything about you, and it is not
sentimental — it is what she calls everybody under sixty.

---

## Marek

Made you in 1931. Old — he does not say how old and the number he gives is a
different one each century. Patient. He does not come onto the property.

He stands at the chain-link fence under the sodium light for seven hours and
talks to you through the glass of the outer doors, and everything he says is
true. That is what makes him dangerous. He will tell you that you were dying;
that he gave you ninety-four years; that the church is a room; that Verna will
be dead in six years anyway; and that nothing in here has ever once answered
you.

He wants you back because you left, and because leaving is a thing the others
must not learn is possible.

---

## Fr. Dan Cusack

Thirty-four. Four parishes. Arrives at **5:40 a.m.** to open up and say the 6:30,
because Thursday is the day he does St. Wenceslaus. Badly slept, unshaven, a
travel mug in one hand and a set of keys he has not sorted since June.

He is not a warrior priest. He is a tired diocesan priest who will do his job,
which is the point.

## The four voices

Written before the dialogue was revised. Read each character's lines end to end
and cut anything that could have come out of somebody else's mouth.

**Marek — learned it as an adult, from books, a very long time ago.** He has
no idioms at all; idioms are the last thing you acquire and he never did. Word
order is a shade formal and occasionally a shade wrong in a way that is not
quite placeable. He counts things — dates, street names, sums, minutes — because
he keeps records and that is how he thinks. No metaphor: he does not have that
gear. He answers a direct question completely, including when the answer costs
him. **Never says:** anything he cannot source, and never a figure of speech.

**Verna — Braddock, eighth grade, mother from Trenčín.** Four to nine words a
sentence. Talks in what things cost and what they weigh. Two Pittsburgh
markers, used and not explained: a thing *needs washed*, and you *redd up* a
room. Her mother's English is under hers in the rhythm, not the spelling.
Answers with a fact and then undercuts it with a second sentence. *"Well."* as a
whole utterance. Calls him *son*. **Never says:** an abstract noun. There is no
*grace*, no *mercy*, no *redemption* in her — there is *somebody has to*.

**Ondrej — left school at fourteen for the open hearth, 1918.** This binds the
narration as well as the dialogue, because in second person the narration is
his mind. He does not reach for *geometry* or *asymmetrically* as figures of
speech; he reaches for tools, weights, weather, and the trades. Ninety-four
years of reading gave him range and did not give him a different set of
instincts. Aloud he says two to seven words, because he has not used his voice
in a week and everything he might say is either a lie or too big.
**Never says:** what he wants. He cannot, until the register, and then he can.

**Fr. Cusack — modern, thirty-four, four parishes, tired.** *Right.* as a whole
sentence, and *All right* when frightened. Self-interrupts and restarts. Builds
sentences as *I'm going to* — announcing his next act, which is what a man does
when he has no idea what he is doing. Says out loud what he cannot do, before
anybody asks. **Never says:** anything claiming more authority than he has.

## What is true in this world

Rules that hold on every path. Do not contradict them.

1. **Baptism leaves a mark that nothing removes.** That is why you can be inside
   the building at all, why the font burns, and why the lamp is unbearable
   rather than merely unpleasant. You are not a stranger here. You are a son who
   has been away.
2. **The lamp is not a weapon and He is not a weapon.** Nothing in this church
   attacks you. The pain is not punishment; it is what light does to something
   that has lived in a cellar for ninety-four years.
3. **You cannot receive the Sacrament.** You can desire it. That desire has a
   name and is worth something, and it is not a loophole.
4. **Contrition works without a priest** if it is sorrow for having offended
   God rather than fear of the consequences, and if you would confess were a
   priest available. Fr. Cusack arrives at 5:40 and is available for
   thirty-four minutes.
5. **Grace is not a cure.** Nothing tonight makes you human, stops the sun, or
   undoes ninety-four years. It changes what the sunrise means. Never say this
   out loud in the text.
6. **Forcing the tabernacle is the one unforgivable-feeling act in the game**,
   and it is available, and it must be terrible.
