The outer doors of St. Wenceslaus are oak and they are standing open, and you are through them before you have decided anything.

Behind you, Braddock Avenue is wet and empty under the sodium lights, and there are two of them at the corner by the shuttered pharmacy who have been walking behind you since the bridge. They are not hurrying. They have never once had to hurry.

You are in a vestibule with a terrazzo floor and a rack of missalettes and a radiator ticking under the window. It smells of candle wax and wet coats. Somewhere ahead, past a second set of doors with glass in them, there is a red light burning that you can see from here and cannot look at.

You have not needed to breathe since April of 1931.

You are breathing anyway, hard, because the room expects it.

*page_break

Between you and the inner doors there is a granite bowl on a granite pillar.

It is knee-high and it is directly in the way, because whoever built this place in 1897 put it exactly where a person coming in from the street would have to pass it. That was the point. Somebody filled it on Saturday and nobody has emptied it, and the surface of the water is perfectly still, and there is a hairline crack in the granite that somebody patched once with the wrong colour of grout.

The vestibule is four paces across. The bowl takes up one of them.

You go along the wall.

The radiator is on and it is the first warmth you have felt in a week, and you are aware of how absurd it is that you notice. Your left sleeve brushes the pillar. That is all. There is a distance of perhaps forty centimetres between your coat and the water and you keep it, and you get through the inner doors with nothing worse than the feeling of having been looked at by something that did not turn its head.

You go straight past it, close enough that your coat swings over the rim.

It is not a burn. Everybody says burn, and it is not that. It is the feeling of a name being said in a room you are standing in, when you had been sure nobody there knew it — that lurch under the sternum, the whole body going still and hot at once.

You are on your knees on the terrazzo before you understand that you have gone down.

It lasts nine seconds. When it passes you are looking at the underside of the bowl from twenty centimetres away, at the place where the granite was patched with the wrong grout, and at a shape cut into the pillar's base that you can just make out in the dark: a year. **1897.**

You get up. Your hands are shaking, and they have not shaken in a very long time.

*label nave_back

The nave of St. Wenceslaus is twenty-two pews on each side and one light left on, over the stair to the choir loft, and everything else in the building is dark except at the far end.

At the far end, up three steps and behind a rail that nobody ever got around to taking out, there is a small brass box in the wall, and above it, hanging on a chain from the ceiling, a wick burning in red glass.

It has been burning since 1897. It goes out only when the box is empty, and the box has not been empty in a hundred and twenty-eight years except once, in a power cut in 1966, and the woman who was here that night is here tonight.

You cannot look at it. Not the way you cannot look at the sun — the way you cannot look at somebody you did something to.

You take the last pew on the left and sit down in a church for the first time since Coolidge was president.

And here is the thing you cannot account for, then or afterwards: you did not have to sit down.

There is a choir loft over your head, a tower stair behind the curtain, a sacristy with a window in it and a basement with no windows at all. Any of those would hide a man from a car park. You have chosen instead the one bench in the building from which you can see the far end of it, and you have sat down facing that way, and you are not going to move.

You came in here because you were driven in. You are still sitting here for some other reason and you do not have a word for it, and you have not had a word for it since you were seven years old.

You look up the length of the nave at the red glass.

Four seconds, and you are looking at the floor again and your jaw is locked so hard that something in it grinds. There is no light in this building bright enough to hurt anybody. The one over the choir stair is a forty-watt bulb. That is not what happened.

What happened is that somebody was in the room and had been the whole time, and you had been behaving as though the room were empty, and for four seconds you were not able to keep that up.

You sit very still until it stops.

You keep your eyes down.

There is a hymn number card on the back of the pew in front, still showing last Sunday's numbers, and a cracked leather kneeler, and a hole in the wood where somebody's grandson worked at it with a pencil forty years ago. You look at all of that instead.

It does not help as much as you expected. You can feel where the far end of the building is the way you can feel where a stove is in a dark kitchen.

*goto verna_speaks

*label verna_speaks
*page_break

Then the fourth pew on the other side says: "Well."

*page_break

She has turned round on the bench with one arm over the back of it, the way you turn round in a car, and she is looking straight at you. Seventy-nine or eighty. A clear plastic rain bonnet still tied under her chin. A thermos on the seat beside her and a rosary in her right hand with the beads of one decade worn down to the string.

She has been here since eleven. She is here every Thursday from eleven to midnight and has been for thirty-one years, since the parish signed up for perpetual adoration in 1994 and the roster fell apart inside two years and nobody told her.

She is frightened of you. You can hear it from four metres away: the heart going, the small catch in the breath. She is frightened in the complete physical way that an animal is frightened, and she has not moved, and she is not going to.

She has also, in the eleven seconds since you sat down, worked out what you are. You can watch her do it. Her eyes go to the font behind you, and then to the width of the gap you left going round it, and then to your hands; and then something in her face closes over, the way a ledger closes.

Her mother came out of a village forty kilometres from Trenčín in 1921 with one small vocabulary of things you do not say out loud after dark, and the word for what you are is in it. Verna Sowka has not believed that word since she was about eleven years old.

She believes it now. It took her eleven seconds and she has not screamed and she has not run, and you have been doing this since 1931 and have never once had either of those things not happen.

"You came in fast," she says. "Is somebody after you?"

"Yes," you say.

Your own voice surprises you. It is a week since you used it and it comes out wrong — too quiet, and with the flat vowels of a mill town that stopped talking like that forty years ago.

Verna Sowka looks at you for a while.

"Then sit further in," she says. "Anybody at that door can see you where you are."

You say nothing.

It goes on a long time. She does not fill it, which is not what you expected; she just keeps her arm over the back of the pew and looks at you, and her heart goes, and after a while she nods slightly at something she has worked out on her own.

"All right," she says. "Then sit further in. Anybody at that door can see you where you are."

The stair to the choir loft is behind a curtain by the inner doors and it takes you up eleven steps to a gallery with an organ console under a dust sheet and four rows of benches nobody has sat in since the parish stopped having a choir.

From up here you can see the whole nave, the red lamp at the end of it, and the top of a clear plastic rain bonnet in the fourth pew on the Mary side.

She has not turned back round. She is still looking at where you were.

"You can stay up there if you like," she says, in a normal voice, to the empty pew you left. "It's warmer down here and the bench is padded, and I'm not going anywhere until midnight."

You come down. You are not sure why, and you will think about it later.

*label after_verna

You move up four pews. It is not much and it puts a pillar between you and the glass of the inner doors, and she watches you do it and then turns back round to face the front, which is the thing that undoes you slightly — that she turns her back.

Out in the lot, under the sodium light on the pole by the fence, someone is standing.

He is not moving. He has his hands in his coat pockets and his head tipped slightly, the way a man stands when he is waiting for somebody to finish an argument he already knows the end of. He does not come any closer than the chain-link fence, and he will not come closer all night, and you know exactly why and it is not the reason people think.

His name is Marek. He is the one who made you, in a boarding house on Talbot Avenue in April of 1931, and he has not raised his voice to you in ninety-four years.

He is not here to kill you. He is here to take you home, which he regards as the same errand and a kindness, and he is probably right about the second part.

The two at the corner by the shuttered pharmacy are the ones who would do the other thing, if it came to that. Marek has never done that part himself. He stands at fences and waits, and in ninety-four years you have not once seen it fail to work.

*page_break

At three minutes to midnight Verna Sowka gets up, badly, holding the pew in front of her, and walks the whole length of the nave past you and out into the vestibule.

You hear the outer doors close. You hear the bolt go over. You hear the key turn, twice, and then a small sound of a key being put into a coat pocket.

She comes back and stands at the end of your pew with her hand on the rail.

"They lock at midnight," she says. "I do it, because I'm the last one out on Thursdays." She is still frightened. It has not gone down at all in the last twenty minutes; if anything it is worse, now that she is close enough to see your hands. "Only I'm not going out. Not with that one standing in the lot."

She sits down on the end of the pew in front of yours, sideways, so she can see you.

"Sun's up at ten past six," she says. "I looked it up on the phone. Fourteen minutes past, if you want it exact."

*page_break

She says it the way you would say a bus time.

She has locked the two of you in together on purpose. She is seventy-nine, her hip is bad, and the nearest door she could reach is forty metres away behind a bolt she threw herself.

You ask her whether she understands what she has just done. It is the only question in the building.

"I'm not stupid, son." She pulls her coat straight over her knees. "I've a bad valve. Aortic. They told me in June to get my affairs in order and I told them I've had my affairs in order since nineteen ninety-eight." She says it exactly the way she said the sunrise time. "So there isn't a great deal you can threaten me with that isn't already on the calendar."

"That is not the same as being safe."

"No," Verna agrees. "It isn't. I'm frightened half to death and I'd be obliged if you didn't make me say that twice." She looks down the length of the nave at the red glass. "But somebody has to sit with you. There's nobody else in the building and I'm the one that's here, and I've been telling Him for thirty-one years that I'd take whatever He sent on a Thursday."

She puts her hands back in her lap.

"Six hours and something," she says. "I'd usually be home by now."

"Open that door and go home," you say. "I am asking you. You do not understand what is in this pew."

"I understand fine." She works the thermos cap loose with both hands, because her hands are not good either. "You came in off the street with your coat on and you went past that font like it bit you, and then you sat in the back like a man at his own mother's funeral." She pours an inch of tea into the cap. "I've been coming here thirty-one years, son. I know what people look like when they've come in to get away from something. It's most of them."

"And the one in the lot?"

"That one didn't come in," Verna says.

"Why would you do that?" you say.

She thinks about it properly, which very few people do when you ask them a real question.

"Because I've had this hour thirty-one years and nobody has ever needed it," she says at last. "Not once. I sat here every Thursday from eleven to twelve and it was me and Him and the radiator." She gets the thermos cap loose with both hands. "And tonight there's somebody in the back of my church at eleven o'clock at night with his coat on, and he's been shaking since the vestibule."

She pours an inch of tea into the cap.

"So I'm not going out to the car park. Don't ask me twice, because the second time I'd have to think about it."
