Twenty-five to six. The car is a fifteen-year-old Corolla with a diocesan parking sticker and one hubcap missing, and it parks across two spaces because there is nobody to park badly in front of.

The man who gets out is thirty-four, unshaven, in a black jacket over a clerical shirt with the collar tab pushed in crooked. He has a travel mug in one hand and a ring of keys in the other that he has not sorted since June, and he stands under the sodium light going through them with his thumb.

Marek has stepped back from the fence. He is nine metres away in the dark by the hedge and Fr. Dan Cusack walks past him without seeing him, because Marek has been not being seen since before the mill was built.

The side door goes. The corridor light comes on.

*page_break

"Verna," says Fr. Cusack, from the sacristy door, in the flat voice of a man who has found something he has to deal with before coffee. "It's twenty to six."

"I know what time it is, Father."

"You've been here since eleven."

"I have."

He comes out past the rail with the mug still in his hand, and he gets four steps into the sanctuary before he sees you, halfway up the aisle in a coat, forty metres from the nearest working light.

He stops.

*page_break

To his enormous credit he does not run and he does not shout. What he does is put the mug down on the second step, which is not where mugs go, and say: "All right."

Then: "Verna. Come here to me."

"No," says Verna Sowka, from the fourth pew. "You come here to us, and sit down, and I'll tell you what's what."

*page_break

It takes four minutes and she does it badly, and it does not matter, because by the second minute Fr. Cusack has stopped listening to her and started looking at you properly — at where you are on the floor, at how far up the aisle you got, at the fact that you have not once in four minutes looked at the far end of the building.

He is not a man who believes in this. Nothing in nine years of seminary and four parishes has prepared him for it and he does not, in fact, believe it, and you can hear his heart going like a bird's.

He gets up off the pew and comes down the aisle to you and stops two metres away.

"Sunrise is fourteen minutes past six," he says. "Is that right?"

Fr. Cusack finds a wet floor, a cracked font on its side, and a coat.

He kneels down in the water in his good trousers and he stays there a long time, and when the diocese asks him afterwards what happened he says, every time, the same sentence, which is: *A man came in out of the rain and I was late.*

You cannot receive. You have known that since the vestibule and it has never once been in question tonight: not the box, not the aisle, not the rail. There is no version of this where you go up those three steps and are given anything.

What you can do is want it.

That has a name, and the name is older than this building and older than the country the building is in, and a man from Trenčín taught it to a woman who taught it to a boy in 1911 in a house on Talbot Avenue: that when you cannot come to the table you can ask to be brought, and the asking is not a substitute for anything, and it is not nothing.

Nine minutes, and nothing to do with them.

You have not said anything to anybody tonight. Not in the box, not on the aisle, not to the man who sat in a wooden cupboard for twenty-nine minutes on the off chance. You have been in this building for seven hours and you have been perfectly silent in the only direction that was open.

It is not defiance. You would have to care more than you do for it to be defiance. It is ninety-four years of a habit, and the habit is holding, and you can feel it holding right up to the end.

At fourteen minutes past six the sun comes down Braddock Avenue and through the east window.

It is a fire. That is all, and it is quick, and there is no part of it that is addressed to anybody.
