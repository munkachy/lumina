Twenty past four. The aisle is forty metres of terrazzo with the wax worn off down the middle where a hundred and twenty-eight years of people have walked.

You get about halfway.

It is not a wall and there is nothing dramatic about it. It is the way a room feels when you have wronged the person sitting in it and they have not said anything, and the wrongness is entirely in you, and every step forward is a step further into being seen.

At the halfway point you are on your knees on the terrazzo, and you are not sure how many pews you have passed.

You want two things and they are pulling in opposite directions on the same rope, and you have wanted both of them since you came in off Braddock Avenue.

You want to be nearer. That is the one you cannot explain and have not been able to explain since the vestibule.

And you want it to stop — the lamp, the room, the being-seen — and the fastest way to make a thing stop being there is the way you have made things stop being there since 1931.

From here you can see the whole sanctuary: three steps, the rail nobody removed, the altar, and behind it in the wall a brass box the size of a bread bin with a small key in a lock that has not been turned since Sunday, and above it the lamp.

The box is not locked well. You could open it in a second and a half. You have opened better than that.

Up here the lamp is not at the far end of anything. It is above your head and slightly behind you, and the red comes down over your shoulders and onto your hands and onto the brass door of the box, which is warm, because it is under a lamp.

You turn the key. The brass door opens the way an old lock opens, which is easily.

Inside there is a linen cloth and a covered vessel, and inside that there is bread, and it is not bread.

You knew that before you touched the door. That is the thing that will never afterwards be arguable: you did not do this because you thought it was empty. You did it because it was full, and because you have spent ninety-four years being unable to bear that anything was full.

What you want, when your hand closes, is to *have* Him.

That is the whole of it and it is not complicated. For ninety-four years you have taken what you wanted into yourself and it has always worked, every time, without exception, and this is the one thing you have ever wanted that you have not been able to take.

It does not work. It cannot be made to work; a person is not had that way, and the wanting turns over in your hands while you are still holding it. By the time your hand comes down you have stopped trying to have Him and started trying to make Him not be there — and the two turn out to be one thing in different coats, and the one thing is taking.

And then nothing happens to you.

That is the sentence to take away from this. *Nothing happens to you.* No fire, no voice, no hand out of the ceiling. You are not struck down. You are not even prevented. You do the worst thing available to anything in this world, on the second step, under a lamp, and the building lets you — the way a man stands still and lets his son hit him.

Fr. Dan Cusack finds the sanctuary at twenty to six. He is thirty-four years old and he has four parishes and he has never had a day like it, and what he does — before the police, before the diocese, before anything — is kneel down on the second step and start to gather up what is on the floor with his hands.

You are in the culvert under the Rankin Bridge by six.

You have another ninety-four years, if you want them. You will spend a good many of them working out what kind of thing it is that could have stopped you and did not, and every answer you arrive at will be worse than the one before.
