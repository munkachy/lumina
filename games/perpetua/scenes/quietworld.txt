Ossa is a rock.

That is the entire catalogue entry, forty-one years old, written by a Survey lieutenant who spent nine hours in orbit and had somewhere else to be: *OSSA. Terrestrial, 0.81 standard. Atmosphere thin, inert. Surface ice and basalt. No biology. No resources of interest. Recommend no further visit.*

You are here because the Survey updates its catalogue every forty years whether anybody wants it or not, and because after Sedge and the *Corvine* and Article Nine, a fortnight in orbit around a rock is the best thing that could possibly happen to this ship.

For nine days nothing happens. It is wonderful. ${engname} strips the number two coolant loop for the fourth time and fails to stop it whistling. ${medname} runs the annual physicals and finds that eleven people have gained weight and nobody has lost any, which ${medthey} reports to you as *the single most encouraging medical finding of my career.*

On the tenth day, at 0410, Tey Abara calls the bridge.

*comment ---------------- The note ----------------

Abara is twenty-two. She talked a sixteen-year-old about Sarn for two hours at the *Corvine* and did not hand over. She has been on the graveyard comms watch for five months and she is, by an enormous margin, the most bored person on this ship.

"Captain," she says, "there's something on the hull."

It is not on the hull. It is coming *through* it: a sound at the very bottom of hearing, so low that half the bridge cannot hear it at all and the other half can feel it in the teeth. It is not machinery. ${engname} shuts down every rotating component on the ship in eleven minutes to prove it, and the sound goes on.

It is the planet.

*page_break

Ossa rings.

Every eleven days, when the gas giant it orbits pulls it into its longest stretch, the whole crust flexes; and forty kilometres down, in a network of ice caverns that runs the entire circumference of the world, the flex drives a column of air, and the world sounds a single note and holds it for sixty hours.

It is B flat, three octaves below the bottom of a piano. It has been going for somewhere between two hundred and four hundred million years. Nobody has ever heard it, because the Survey lieutenant who catalogued Ossa was here on day four of an eleven-day cycle and had somewhere else to be.

*comment ---------------- The probe ----------------

Within three days the *Perpetua* has become a research vessel, which she was designed to be and has not been for fourteen months, and it is the happiest the ship has been.

The science party wants to put a probe down.

It is a good proposal. Forty kilometres of ice, a bore of eleven centimetres, an instrument package into the cavern network itself: pressure, temperature, gas composition, the geometry of a resonant chamber the size of a continent. Nobody has ever measured anything like it. It would be the most significant thing the *Perpetua* has done in twenty-six years and it would carry your name on it.

And then, on the fifth day, the science party finds the problem and reports it honestly, which is to their enormous credit.

An eleven-centimetre bore into a resonant chamber vents. Not much: a fraction of a per cent of the column, forever, because ice at that depth does not re-seal. The note would not stop. It would go flat — by something on the order of one part in nine thousand, permanently, for the remaining life of the planet.

Nobody would ever hear the difference. No instrument in the Concord could hear the difference without the *before* recording, which only this ship has, which you made at 0410 on a Tuesday.

*comment ---------------- Counsel's moment of glory ----------------

The forty-first submission — or, if there were no submissions, a request to see the master, made properly through the first officer, and waited out for two days — is from Tey Abara, comms rating, twenty-two, who has no scientific qualification whatsoever.

It is one page. It is not sentimental. It does not use the word *beautiful* and it does not ask you to spare anything.

What it says is this. The Survey has forty-one years of archived gravitational data on Ossa, taken from four passing ships, and Ossa's flex cycle is recorded in all four. The note's frequency is a function of the cavern geometry. The cavern geometry is a function of the flex. Therefore the drift in the note across forty-one years is already in the archive, and from the drift you can solve the geometry, and you can have almost everything the probe would give you for the price of a fortnight's arithmetic and no hole at all.

She has done nine pages of the arithmetic in the back. Three of the nine pages are wrong.

Six of them are not.
