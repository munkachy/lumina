*comment ---------------- Ovan Rask ----------------

Kem Station is lit up like a city and leaking from four points along its spine, and the whole of it is turning very slowly, and there is a plume off the number one separator hall that goes out sixty kilometres before it thins.

**Ovan Rask** is the station chief. He is forty-four, he has been here eleven years, and he is on your screen inside two minutes with soot on his face.

"Commander. Thank you for coming. We are not evacuating."

*comment ---------------- The hall ----------------

The number one separator hall is on fire in a way that is not really fire: a hydrogen flame at that pressure is nearly invisible and it cuts.

The problem is a valve. There is a manual isolator eleven metres inside the hall, behind the gallery, which was designed to be reachable and is now on the wrong side of a plume, and if somebody gets to it the hall stops burning in ninety seconds and the refinery is saved. If nobody gets to it, the hall burns for another four hours and takes the spine with it.

Rask's fire chief will not send anyone. He is right not to.

The number one separator hall burns for four hours and eleven minutes and then takes the spine.

Kem Station does not explode; refineries do not explode, they come apart in a slow and undramatic way over a day and a half, venting, while everybody watches from a Survey ship's holds. By the third day it is eleven thousand tonnes of scrap in a decaying orbit and the Concord will spend four years arguing about who pays to clear it.

Four hundred and eleven people are alive. Every single one.

Ninety thousand people on the moon below will burn stored hydrogen through a four-month winter, and then rationed hydrogen through the next one, and the Concord will get a temporary plant up in the second year. The excess mortality figure, when it is published eleven months later, is two hundred and forty.

Ovan Rask does not speak to you again.
