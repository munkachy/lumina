One Soul

You are an angel, and one life is yours to keep.

Target 26,000 words Choices 154 Scenes 13 Ward Adam Kowalczyk, 1975–2024

Draft 4 puts a second person in the room, and it changes everything. There is now a woman who watches all of this, and the angel’s orders reach him through her decisions. That single move solves the discursive problem outright rather than working around it, gives the game its comedy, and turns the whole thing into her story as much as Adam’s.

00The frame

Loretta Boyd is seventy-one years old, and on a Tuesday in October 2024 she sweeps fourteen porcelain angels off a windowsill with her forearm.

She is in a rehabilitation facility in Pittsburgh, four days after her second amputation. Diabetes took the left leg in 2019 and the right one last week. She has been a member of St. Benedict the Moor in the Hill District for fifty years. She has prayed to her guardian angel every single day of her adult life — out loud, by a name she made up, in the way people actually do it — and the statues were gifts, from her sister and her sodality and her late husband, accumulated over four decades. Every one of them is a woman in a nightgown with a harp.

None of them looks anything at all like what she is about to meet.

The mechanic

She is taken up — the game does not over-explain how, and Paul’s account of a man caught up and shown things he could not afterwards put into words is the only gesture it makes at it — and shown the creation of the angels, and their one choice, and then the whole of one ordinary man’s life from 1975 to 2024.

And she is not a spectator. For the duration, God’s command to the angel reaches him through her decisions.

So every choice in this game is hers. The angel does not deliberate, does not weigh options, does not reason his way toward a conclusion. He waits, receives, and acts — freely, out of love, exactly as he always has. The only difference is where the command comes from.

Why this is not bad theology

The obvious objection is that God appears to be taking orders from a woman in a rehab facility. He is not, and the distinction matters enough to state plainly.

She is a secondary cause, which is what everybody is

God is not learning anything from her and is not constrained by her. He is using her choosing as the means by which the command arrives — which is what He does with every creature that has ever done anything (ST I q.105 a.5: God works in every agent). A priest absolves and God forgives through him. A prophet speaks and God speaks through him. The creature’s act is genuinely the creature’s and genuinely free; God’s act is genuinely God’s; and they are not in competition because they are not on the same level of causality.

She is fallible, and that is the whole point

God has not made her an oracle. He has given her real latitude inside a permitted range — and her choices are hers, and some of them are going to be wrong, and the game absolutely depends on that being true. Providence encompasses a wrong choice without endorsing it.

The Rule already covers this exactly

An abbot’s command is obeyed as though it came from God, and the abbot can still be wrong, and he will answer for it (RB 2). Even a command beyond a monk’s strength is attempted first and represented afterwards (RB 68). The obedience is real, the fallibility is real, and God’s will is done through both without either being cancelled.

For forty-nine years, Loretta is the abbot. She does not know this and would not have volunteered.

It is extraordinary, and the game says so

The angel states plainly, early, that this is not how it normally goes — that he is ordinarily moved by an interior light and that this arrangement exists once, for her, because of what she did to the windowsill. Zechariah was struck mute for unbelief. Paul was put on the ground and shown what he could not repeat. Severe interventions that are mercies are not a new idea and she is not the first person to be handled roughly and to her benefit.

What it fixes

The discursive problem is gone, not mitigated

The objection was that angels do not reason step by step, so a game about an angel forming theories and being wrong is a game about something that cannot happen.

Now the deliberating happens in a human being, which is where deliberating belongs. Loretta weighs, wonders, guesses and gets things wrong — because she is a woman, and that is what women do, and it takes her time because she is in time. The angel receives what she decides and obeys it instantly, completely, and without weighing anything. He is exactly what Aquinas says he is. She is exactly what we are.

The retrospective frame from earlier drafts is now literal: the whole story is the angel telling it to Loretta, which is why it keeps reaching for human comparisons and why it is shaped like a story at all. The translation is for her. And so is the afterword.

It is also where the comedy lives

The frame breaks, regularly, and the frame breaking is funny.

“Well?” she says. “Do something.”

“I am waiting to be told what to do.”

“By who?”

A pause that she will remember for the rest of her life.

“You.”

She asks, more than once, whether he is sure about this arrangement, and he says that he is, and that he has no opinion about it, and that this is also not a thing he has. So she is given forty-nine years of standing next to somebody she cannot help, holding the only three tools there are, watching a good thing be destroyed slowly by a man she comes to love, with the power to intervene and no permission to — and then she is brought back and asked what she thinks now.

Nobody explains suffering to her. She is made to do the job.

01Five rules against becoming a tract

One · his guilt is never established

Mortal sin needs grave matter, full knowledge, and deliberate consent (CCC 1857). Consent lives in the interior, and nothing created can see in there. CCC 2352 says affective immaturity, force of habit and conditions of anxiety can lessen culpability or reduce it to a minimum. So his column carries a field reading culpability: unknown that never changes once in forty-nine years.

Two · the angel never touches the will

Not once, on any branch. Every tool works on circumstances: put a person in his path, put a book on a table, stand on a suggestion so it does not arrive, be somewhere. One beat where the angel moves Adam’s choice from inside and the whole thing is a puppet show.

Three · no plot punishment

Nothing bad happens to Adam because he did something wrong. Every consequence is intrinsic — the act doing what the act does, visible in how he treats one specific woman. That distinction is the whole thesis.

Four · every argument at full strength — including the relief

His question at eighteen gets written at full power. So does the philosophy at twenty-two. And so does the freedom. When he stops going to Mass, the relief is real and the game shows it: he is out from under a man with a clipboard, a liturgy that was genuinely bad on Brereton Street in 1992, and a room full of people he had watched be unkind. Some of his reasons for leaving are good reasons. A story where leaving feels only like loss is a story nobody believes.

Five · chastity gets its worst advocate on the page

There is a man in this game who has never once done what Adam does, who is disciplined, correct about every fact, and entirely without mercy. He is one of the reasons Adam leaves — not the priest, not the teaching, a layman with a clipboard. The player has to feel the force of what Adam is rejecting, and it has to be genuinely repellent, or the game is a Chick tract with better typography.

The point is not that chastity is destructive. It is that a virtue held without charity becomes a weapon, and that the person on the receiving end cannot tell the difference between the virtue and the weapon until much later.

02Warmth, comedy, and the birds

The largest risk left in this outline is not theology. It is that a reader will not spend 24,000 words with a man they only ever see failing. If Adam is not somebody you would want to know, none of the rest of it lands.

So a hard production constraint: every scene except 11 and 12 carries at least one beat that is warm, funny, or both, and that does not advance the trial at all. Those beats are not filler. They are what makes the trial cost something.

The pigeons

Stan’s father kept a loft on the roof of the Dobson Street house. It has been empty since 1971. In 1985, the week the mill closes, Stan cleans it out and hands it to Adam without saying much of anything — because he is a man who cannot talk about anything that matters and can give things instead.

Racing homers. Roof lofts were real in that neighbourhood and the clubs still ran into the eighties. Adam is ten, and then thirteen, and then thirty, and the birds are the one continuous thing in his life.

And here is the mechanic. A pigeon has no free will. So the angel’s power over a bird is complete. He can turn it, lift it, bring it home over four hundred miles of Ohio. He can do absolutely nothing about the boy standing on the roof holding the basket.

An angel of God, ninth choir, present at the creation of the world, spending the afternoon of 12 May 1985 getting a hen named Josie back from Springfield. It is funny. It is the only unambiguous competence he has. And it is the closest thing to affection the story is going to allow itself for a very long time.

Never say out loud that a homing pigeon always comes back.

The birds also give the angel somewhere to put Valor and Charity in scenes where he is otherwise helpless — small, real, low-stakes wins that build stats and buy the player goodwill.

03What the angel is not

The frame does the heavy lifting now — the deliberating happens in Loretta, so the angel never reasons discursively at any point in this game. But three supporting facts still govern how every line about him gets written.

He is not slow. He is short of data.

Never write the angel wondered. Write: he saw, instantly and completely, everything that could be seen from outside — and it was not enough. Not knowing whether Adam consented is not a failure of reasoning. It is the absence of an object. God reserved it.

Loretta wonders. He reports. The difference should be audible in every scene.

His obedience is a free act, not a mechanism

Angels choose (ST I q.59 a.3), measured not by our flowing time but by aevum — a succession of complete acts rather than a duration. The world changes; he acts again. Receiving an order and executing it out of love is free, and he must never sound like a machine taking input. He obeys because he wants to. He says so once, flatly, when she asks, and never mentions it again.

He is not wrong. He is uninformed — and that is not a sin.

A confirmed angel cannot be deceived about what he knows, but can be ignorant of what has not been given, including free future acts (ST I q.57 a.3, q.58 a.5). So when a choice turns out badly, Loretta was mistaken; he was obedient. Nothing in this game requires an angel to sin and nothing in it should read as though it does.

On 1 Corinthians 6:3 — “know ye not that we shall judge angels?” — the standard reading, and Aquinas’s, concerns the fallen. The good angels are confirmed and are not on trial. Loretta asks about this once, in scene 1, and gets exactly that answer.

The afterword — the last thing in the game

After the threshold, after the stat chart, after everything: about a hundred and fifty words in the angel’s voice — and now they are spoken to Loretta, in the room, before she is sent back. This is where the love goes.

He says that he has told it the way you tell things, and that it did not happen like that. That he did not wonder about the boy for three days in 1962 — that he saw all of it at once and the all of it was not enough, and there is no word in any human language for that difference. That the shape of the story is a translation, made so it could be carried.

And that the one thing he has not translated, because it does not translate, is why he stayed.

No sentiment, no adjectives, no address to God. The love is expressed as staying and as the untranslatable part — and he is saying it to a woman who stopped believing anybody stays, four days after they took her second leg.

05The stat sheet

Three opposed pairs and two free-standing virtues. Hope is new, and it came directly out of your note about the angel loving a soul he has stopped expecting much from.

ValorPatience

Move now, or keep the long watch.

SeverityMercy

How you stand next to a failure. Both are real virtues; the wrong one at fifteen does thirty years of damage.

SplendorHiddenness

Manifest, or work entirely through ordinary means — a late bus, a friend who calls, a book left face up.

Charitynot opposed

Whether you still love him. This one is very hard to lose.

Hopenot opposed

Whether you still expect anything of him. Much easier to lose than Charity, and losing it is not a sin. It is damaged by the blind years, by the demons’ bragging, and — if you listened to him in the court — by a reading you accepted before you had anything of your own to compare it to.

Why Hope had to be separate

You can love someone completely and still quietly stop expecting anything of them. That is a real thing people do to each other, it is not the same as ceasing to love, and it is not a sin for the angel — hope about a free future act is not knowledge, and a creature is allowed to have less of it than it should.

It is also the stat the endings are keyed to, because the last scene of this game is an act of hope, and how much of it you have left after forty-nine years is the thing the player has actually been playing for.

06The blind years

Your best note, and it changes the shape of the whole middle of the game.

The angel cannot see him

In the hour of grave sin, the angel’s sight of Adam goes. Not a punishment and not a withdrawal — something closes, and where the boy was there is a place the angel’s attention will not enter, and he is standing four feet from it and cannot get in.

He panics. The first time, in 1989, an angel who watched a third of creation throw itself away without flinching experiences something he has no name for, and the narration does not name it either.

And on the other side of the dark, they are delighted. This is the one thing they have that he has not got, and they never stop mentioning it. “You’ve no idea what’s going on in there, have you.” It is the only leverage they will ever have over him and they use it for thirty years.

Status: pious tradition, not doctrine, and the bible should be honest about that. Aquinas holds the guardian never forsakes his charge entirely (ST I q.113 a.6). What the game shows is an occlusion, not an abandonment — the angel never leaves, never once, and says so.

Three things this fixes at once:

The content problem, completely. The narration cannot go into those rooms because the narrator physically cannot. It is not a euphemism. It is the camera being switched off from outside.

The discursive problem. The angel is not reasoning about what he cannot see. He simply cannot see. That is an absence, not a deduction.

The horror. A guardian angel who can be shut out of the room is a far more frightening idea than one who watches, and it gives the demons the only genuine advantage they hold in forty-nine years.

07The people

Everyone gets a virtue and everyone gets a fault, including the ones who do the damage. Nobody in this game is a device.

Loretta Boyd

the player · b. 1953 · the Hill District

Funny, stubborn, and completely without self-pity, which is not the same as being at peace. Fifty years at St. Benedict the Moor. Retired from the county, two grown children, a husband dead in 2011. Prayed to her guardian angel out loud every day of her adult life, by a name she invented in 1979, and meant every word of it.

Has been the centre of her own suffering for forty years and cannot see past it — which is not a moral failing, it is what long pain does to people, and the game treats it that way. Argues instead of asking. Decides fast and defends it afterwards.

She is the one being converted. Scrooge’s story is not about the Cratchits; this one is not about Adam. His life is the instrument, and hers is the subject, and the last decision in the game is hers in her own body in her own room.

Adam Kowalczyk

the ward · 1975–2024

Deadpan funny. Good with old people and hopeless with people his own age. Can fix anything — it comes from Stan — and is genuinely, quietly gifted with the birds. Loyal, which is why what he does to Renee in 1998 reads as a betrayal of himself.

Learned at five that the voice inside can be turned down, and never forgot it. Avoids conflict by leaving. Mistakes a posture for a conviction and holds it for twenty years without re-examining it once.

Halina Kowalczyk

grandmother · 1898–1986

Blunt, dry, superstitious in ways that are charming right up until they are not. Prays for Adam by name every day for eleven years. Is mocked for it in her own kitchen — by Ewa, affectionately, and by a neighbour, less so — and it makes no difference to her whatsoever.

Hard about Ewa, for a specific old reason, and never lets it go. Keeps every rule and loses charity somewhere in the last four years, and the Prayers number starts falling in 1984, two years before she dies — which the player will notice and not be able to explain.

Her fate is never resolved. A woman who did everything right and went hard at the end. The game does not say where she is and does not hint. She is the second unresolved soul, and she is there so the player understands early that keeping the rules is not the same as the thing the rules are for.

Stan Kowalczyk

father · 1937–2016

Gives things instead of saying them. Hands his son the loft in 1985 the week his own life ends. Prays for him daily from November 1993 to March 2016 and never mentions it once.

Says nine words about the whole subject, in a Buick, badly, and closes it permanently for both of them. His silence is not neglect — he was raised by a man who never spoke either — and it does more damage than any cruelty in the story.

Ewa Kowalczyk

mother · b. 1952

Sharp, funny, works the counter at the bakery on Liberty, swears in Polish on the assumption that nobody is listening. Knows considerably more about her son than she ever says.

Manages by not looking. Teases her mother-in-law about the praying because it is easier than the alternative, and after 1986 cannot forgive herself for it.

Renee Sobek

1975– · three doors down

Loud, funny, better than Adam at nearly everything and cheerfully aware of it. Wants to be a nurse and becomes one. Known him since they were nine.

A coward about conflict. In 1997 she knows something is wrong, asks once, gets told nothing is wrong, and lets it go — and she will think about that for twenty years. She is not a victim and the game must never write her as one.

Dennis Wiśniewski

the Holy Name Society · the clipboard

Genuinely chaste, genuinely disciplined, has never in his life done what Adam does, and is right about every single fact. Runs the parish festival and does it well. Nobody has ever seen him cut a corner.

Has no mercy in him at all. Corners Adam in a church hall in 1991 and says a true thing in a way that makes it unusable for thirty years. He is one of the reasons Adam leaves — not the priest, not the teaching, a layman with a clipboard.

He gets one moment of pathos, late and brief, that shows what was done to him at fourteen and by whom. Not redemption. Just enough that the player cannot dismiss him, and has to sit with the fact that the man was not wrong.

Fr. Emeka Nwosu

2018 · ninety seconds

Fifty-ish, Nigerian, in the diocese eleven years, unhurried, funny, and completely unshockable. Nobody ever made him ashamed of this subject, so he can speak about it the way you would speak about a broken arm — seriously, kindly, and without a trace of embarrassment.

He must read as a breath of fresh air, and the reader should want more of him and not get it. He is in the game for one scene, he says the true thing correctly, and Adam has to sit in a car afterwards.

The man at the bar

Oakland · 1997 · unnamed

A graduate student, three drinks in, holding forth. He says one offhand thing about Christian morality being the invention of people who could not do anything else. The philosopher is never named. Adam is not an intellectual and does not read anything; he borrows the sentence, and the culture repeats it back to him for twenty years until it feels like something he worked out himself.

08Legion

They present as many, and the presentation is the tactic. Different names on different days, different voices, handoffs, that’s not my area, you want the other one, I’ll take a message. They borrow big names — Belphegor, Asmodeus, Mammon — and none of them is entitled to any of them.

“My name is Legion: for we are many.”

Mark 5:9 — a boast, and quite possibly a lie, right there in the Gospel

The angel has to work the bureaucracy, which is your note and it is funnier and worse than what I had. To get anything done he is routed, deferred, referred and stonewalled — and every hour of it is an hour he is not standing where he should be. The paperwork is the attack.

In 1994 he counts them. There are three. One does nearly all the work; the other two exist to make it look like a crowd. None of them is Asmodeus and none ever was. Knowing the count does not help him beat them — it helps him stop being impressed, which turns out to be different and better.

And it rhymes with what is happening to Adam, whose justifications get more elaborate every decade while the thing they are justifying never changes size at all. Complication is the tactic. The truth stays small.

09The hidden nature of grace

You named this and it is the right theme for the whole book, so it is now stated as one and every scene is checked against it.

Everything that works in this story is invisible while it is working

An old woman is laughed at in her own kitchen for praying, and it is the reason her grandson is alive in 2018. A man writes a name in a notebook for twenty-three years and dies without mentioning it. A congregation in Wilkinsburg prays for a man they met twice. A priest says ninety seconds of something in a box and never learns what it did. A bird comes home.

Nobody in this game ever sees the effect of the thing they did. Not one person, not once. Only the angel sees it, and he cannot tell anybody, and that is his whole life.

He is five. He has just learned the single most consequential skill of his life, and he learned it in about four seconds, and no adult in the building saw anything happen.

And you know exactly what you are looking at. Not by reasoning — you see the whole of it at once, including what it means, including roughly what it will cost. You know this will take decades to undo and that you have no tool that touches it.

Why it matters this muchThis is the mechanism that lets him ignore his conscience about Renee in 1998 while it is screaming at him about a thought he did not choose. It is not that his conscience is loud in one place and silent in the other. It is that he learned, at five, which knob to turn.

2.5 + 3.7 — REVISED

Halina

1975–1986

She is mocked for praying. Ewa does it affectionately, at the stove, in front of Adam. A neighbour does it less kindly. Halina is entirely unbothered, which is funny, and keeps going, which is the point.

And it is the reason Adam is alive in 2018. Nobody ever tells her. She never finds out. She dies without knowing.

Your note about the intercession of the dead broke the mechanic open, so here is the harder version. The Prayers number starts falling in 1984 — two years before she dies. Nothing in the text explains it. She goes hard about Ewa over something old, and stops forgiving it, and something goes out of her prayer that the number can measure and the player cannot see.

She dies in 1986, unreconciled, having kept every single rule.

And the game never says where she went. Not a hint, not an implication, not a consoling line. She is the second unresolved soul, she is there in the first act so the player learns early that keeping the rules is not the same as the thing the rules are for — and she is the reason the ending’s uncertainty does not feel like a trick when it arrives forty years later.

6.3b — NEW

The church hall

1991 · age sixteen

Dennis Wiśniewski corners him after a Holy Name meeting, by the coffee urn, and talks to him for eleven minutes about purity.

Every fact he says is true. He is not leering, he is not prurient, he has never done any of it himself and it shows. He is disciplined and impressive and completely certain, and there is not one gram of mercy in the entire eleven minutes, and by the end of it a sixteen-year-old has learned that this is what the people who believe it are like.

The player should want to hit him. The player should also be unable to find the sentence he got wrong. That is the whole design of the scene.

His moment, much later2016, at Stan’s funeral. Dennis is seventy-eight and says one thing, briefly, about his own father, and it explains everything and excuses nothing. Adam does not soften. Neither does the game.
6.6 — EXPANDED

The relief

1993 · age eighteen

He stops going, and it is wonderful. The game commits to this completely for about four hundred words.

No more Dennis by the coffee urn. No more standing in a line on Saturday performing an accounting he cannot get right. No more nine o’clock Mass at St. Casimir in 1992, which was genuinely, objectively bad — a cantor who could not sing, a homily read off a photocopy, a congregation of eighty in a building for six hundred. No more watching people who had been unkind to his mother go up and come back with their hands folded.

Some of his reasons are good reasons. The game says so plainly, does not hedge it, and does not sneak a corrective in at the end of the paragraph.

Why this must be hereA story in which leaving feels only like loss is a story nobody believes, and a reader who has ever left anything will close it. The relief has to be real on the page. What the game does instead is wait twenty-three years and let the reader work out what the relief cost — without ever once pointing at it.
10.1b — NEW

Wilkinsburg

2001 · age twenty-six

A work friend takes him to a service. The people are warm and completely sincere and nobody has an angle. They pray for him by name at the end, out loud, and the Prayers number goes up. No asterisk. No comment from the narration. It just goes up, and anyone paying attention will see it.

And on this one subject the room is split straight down the middle. One man speaks about it in terms more frightening than anything Adam ever heard in a Catholic building — genuinely terrifying, and meant kindly. Another treats it as a non-issue, warmly, and is faintly puzzled anyone still worries.

Adam leaves with no category for either of them, and the game offers him none, and offers the player none either. Both men are trying to help. That is the beat.

Why it earns its placeIt stops the game reading as an attack on Protestants and it stops it reading as an attack on the Church. The problem in this story was never which building Adam was in. It was that nobody in any of them said the true thing in a way he could use. Until 2018.

12The four endings

Your note: they cannot all be equally murky or the player will be annoyed, and there has to be something to win. Agreed. Here is the spread — keyed to Hope, the needle’s final position, whether anything got repaired, and what happened in the last four months.

No ending says Adam was damned. The Church has never in two thousand years declared one individual to be in hell — she canonises and has never once done the reverse — and this game will not be the first thing to do it. But the four are not the same, and nobody will mistake the first for the last.

The Homing

needle 4 · hope high · anointed · Renee · the notebook shown

Obviously hopeful. He asked for a priest himself, in June, before he was frightened. The notebook is on the bed. He wrote to Renee five years ago and she wrote back and they were kind to each other. The last thing he says is not about himself. The angel’s reading is warm and the prose is allowed to be warm — and it still stops short of a verdict, because that is not the angel’s to give, and the reader will not feel cheated because there is so obviously something here.

The Late Hour

needle 3–4 · hope moderate · returned out of fear · it held

He came back frightened rather than free, and it held, and holding is not nothing. Six years of going to Mass on time and not knowing why it helped. Real, thin, and enough to hope on — and the angel knows precisely how thin it is, and hopes anyway, and says so.

Unfinished

returned late · nothing repaired · Renee never heard from him

He got back inside the door and ran out of time before he got anywhere else. Nothing was mended. Nobody was written to. Genuinely murky — the angel can point at three things and none of them settles anything, and the last months were mostly about pain management.

The Long Hope

needle stuck at 3 · hope low · forty-nine years of severity · no priest

Obviously bleak, and it still does not say he is lost. It says something considerably harder to sit with: there is very little here. You do not know. You will not know until the resurrection. And you are going to hope anyway, for all of it, for as long as that takes.

Hope is a virtue, not a guess. That distinction is the last thing the game teaches and it does not explain it.

And then hers

The four above are readings of Adam. The game does not end on any of them, because Adam was never the subject.

She is sent back, and nothing about her has changed

Same room. Same window. Same parking structure. Fourteen porcelain angels in pieces on the floor and a nurse who has not come in yet, because no time has passed at all.

She does not get her legs back. Nothing is fixed. Scrooge does not wake up younger — he wakes up on the same Christmas morning in the same house with the same money, and the only thing in the world that is different is him.

And then she is told the one thing she was not shown: that the man she has just spent forty-nine years standing beside is four doors down this corridor, and has about nine hours left, and that nobody is with him.

The last choice in the game

She has no legs. Going down that corridor means ringing for a nurse she has been rude to all week, being got into a chair, and being wheeled to the room of a man she has never met, to sit with him for no reason she can explain to anybody.

It is genuinely difficult and it is entirely up to her. The game offers it once, in about forty words, with no argument attached, and it accepts either answer without comment.

If she goes: she cannot see the angel. She has stood next to him for forty-nine years and she is now in the same room as him and there is nothing there at all — and she knows exactly where he is standing, because it is where he always stands. She does not say anything to Adam. She would not know what to say. She sits down, and puts her hand on the bed, and that is the end of the game.

If she does not go: the game does not punish her, does not editorialise, and does not withhold the afterword. It gives her the same hundred and fifty words, in the same voice, and lets the reader sit with what they chose. Nothing in this game is ever a trap.

Either way, the last thing on the screen is the corridor, and the door, and the light under it.

Then the rose window, filled. Three pairs, Charity and Hope, and beside it his column — the needle, the years, the prayers, and culpability: unknown, which has not changed since 1975 and does not change now. That chart is the only verdict the player is ever given, and it is not a verdict on him.

And then the afterword, and the untranslatable part, and it ends.

13Open questions

  1. One change I made against your note.You suggested the Pharisee figure be extremely effeminate. I did not do that, and I want to be straight about why: it would read as a cheap shot, and it would give the player an easy reason to dismiss him — which destroys the thing you actually wanted, that Adam’s rejection be sympathetic. Dennis is far more dangerous as a disciplined, impressive, entirely correct man with no mercy in him. That is the real pastoral failure, it is instantly recognisable to anyone who grew up in a parish, and nobody can wave it away. Overrule me if you disagree.
  2. Halina’s unresolved fate — is it one unresolved soul too many?It is a strong move and it does real work: it plants the ending’s uncertainty in act one, and it makes the point about rules without charity without a word of comment. The risk is that two unresolved souls reads as a tic rather than a theme. I think it holds because hers is quiet and never referred to again.
  3. Does the blind mechanic cost too much?Going dark solves modesty, discursion and horror in one move — but it also means the player spends chunks of scenes 6, 8 and 10 unable to see the thing the scene is about. That is the intended experience. It may also be genuinely unpleasant to play. Worth watching in the first draft of scene 6.
  4. Loretta’s particulars are swappable.I made her Black, seventy-one, and a fifty-year member of St. Benedict the Moor in the Hill District — which is a real and historically significant Black Catholic parish, and it means the game’s two central people come from the two Pittsburghs that mostly never met, and meet once, on one floor, in 2024. That is texture rather than theme and I have kept it out of the argument entirely. Change any of it freely.
  5. Does the frame swallow the game?The risk is that Loretta becomes so interesting that the player resents cutting back to 1991. The defence is rationing: she is fully present in scene 0 and scene 12, and everywhere in between she gets six frame breaks and no more. If the first draft of scene 6 wants a seventh, that is the warning sign.
  6. Does she ever learn what she got wrong?Currently: only where the story shows her. There is no accounting scene, no tallying of her bad calls, and no rebuke. I think that is right — the point of the forty-nine years is that she has already felt it — but the alternative, one short exchange before she is sent back, is available and would be brutal.
